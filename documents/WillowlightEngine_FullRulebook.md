# PART ONE: PLAY THE GAME

_Everything you need for tonight_

## WHAT IS THIS GAME?

_\[ART: Two players at a table, one with a screen, one mid-gesture — warmly lit, no fantasy kitsch\]_

The Willowlight Engine is a tabletop roleplaying game. One person at the table — the Storyguide, or SG — describes a world and plays every character in it who isn't a player character. Everyone else plays one character: a person with history, skills, and a stake in what happens.

You play by talking. The SG describes a situation. You say what your character does. Most of the time, that's enough. The fiction moves forward through conversation, no dice required.

When the outcome is uncertain and the stakes matter, you roll dice. The dice don't decide your story. They decide what your story costs.

### The Storyguide

The SG's job is to make the world feel real and to play every character in it who isn't a player character — the innkeeper who shortchanges travelers, the ancient power threatening the region, and everyone between. The SG sets the difficulty of challenges, controls opposition, and describes consequences.

The SG is not the players' adversary. The SG is the world's voice. A good SG presents genuine challenges, plays characters honestly, and lets consequences follow naturally from choices. The fiction should surprise the SG as often as it surprises everyone else.

### The Players

Each player controls one character — a person who exists in the world the SG describes. You decide what your character does, says, and feels. You roll dice when your character attempts something difficult. Your choices shape the story.

Your character is not a blank slate waiting for adventure. They are already someone — a person with a vocation, a history, and a reason to be where the story starts. The adventure is hard because the world is hard, not because your character is fragile.

### The Four Core Principles

The Willowlight Engine runs on four principles that apply at every table, in every session, regardless of setting.

### The Cost of Choosing Your Domain

Every approach to a problem carries consequences beyond the dice. Drawing a blade in a courtroom ends the legal proceeding and starts a criminal one. Publicly destroying someone's reputation creates enemies who remember. Probing someone's mind without consent is experienced as a violation — and the world notices.

The engine does not mechanically restrict what you attempt. The fiction has opinions about it. The SG expresses those opinions through the difficulty of the roll before you make it. If an action is contextually absurd, the difficulty should reflect that before dice hit the table — not after.

If someone asks "can I do this?" the answer is almost always yes. If they ask "should I?" — that is the interesting question.

### Examples, Not Prescriptions

This book provides menus, defaults, and examples throughout — not mandates. When a track fills and a character breaks, the system offers suggested outcomes. When a complication fires, the system offers a menu. The table is always invited to substitute, combine, or invent outcomes that serve the story at hand. The system provides structure and inspiration. The table tells the story.

### The World Remembers

Actions become known. Not immediately, not always completely — but eventually and inevitably, unless someone works to prevent it. Secrecy is not the default state of the world. It is a choice that costs effort, resources, and attention, and that cost compounds the longer the secret is kept.

Words and deeds are carried by witnesses, rumor, and in a world with magic, sometimes by stranger things still. A diviner with the right ability and the right question can find answers in places no human witness survived to give them.

This is not a punishment mechanic. It is a description of how the fiction works.

**_\[SIDEBAR: The world assumes. Silence is not free.\]_**

_Secrecy is a choice with a cost. That cost compounds. The world has a long memory and many eyes. Plan accordingly._

_\[END SIDEBAR\]_

### You Begin as Someone

Starting characters are capable, notable people — not apprentices waiting for their first real adventure. Your vocation is who you already are. Your conviction and path reflect a person who has lived enough to have beliefs worth playing.

This does not mean every character is a hero. A character whose vocation is pig farmer is a genuinely exceptional pig farmer. What it means is that characters begin with agency, capability, and a defined place in the world. The system never asks you to earn the right to be interesting.

## THE ROLL

_\[ART: Three dice — two standard, one visually distinct Luck Die, mid-roll on a dark surface\]_

The Willowlight Engine uses six-sided dice (d6). You always roll three at once. Before rolling, set one die apart as the Luck Die — make it visually distinct from the other two, a different color if you have one. The other two are your Result Dice.

Add the two Result Dice together. Add your attribute. Add your highest applicable skill tier bonus. Compare the total to a Target Number set by the SG before the roll. Meet or beat it, and you succeed.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Part of the roll** | **What it is** |
| Result Dice | Two d6. Their total, plus your modifiers, is your result. |
| Luck Die | One d6, kept separate. A resource you can spend — or that the SG can turn against you. |
| Attribute | One of your nine attributes, chosen to match your approach. The SG confirms it before you roll. |
| Skill tier bonus | Your highest applicable Vocation, Interest, or Hobby bonus. Tiers do not stack. |
| Target Number (TN) | The difficulty of the task. Set by the SG before you roll. |

### Attributes

Every character has nine attributes organized across three domains — Physical, Mental, and Social. Attributes are rated 1 to 5. Most starting characters have ratings of 1 to 3.

Each domain has three attributes describing different ways you engage within it:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain** | **Attribute** | **What it governs** |
| Physical | Might | Force, raw power, physical imposition |
| Physical | Reflex | Speed, precision, agility — also governs Physical initiative |
| Physical | Endurance | Resilience and staying power — sets Vitality track size and Physical defense TN |
| Mental | Reason | Analysis, knowledge, intellectual force |
| Mental | Guile | Deception, cunning, misdirection — also governs Mental initiative |
| Mental | Resolve | Mental fortitude and conviction — sets Willpower track size and Mental defense TN |
| Social | Influence | Presence, persuasion, social imposition |
| Social | Poise | Grace, adaptability, social finesse — also governs Social initiative |
| Social | Command | Authority and coordination — sets Composure track size and Social defense TN |

When you make a roll, you choose which attribute fits your approach and the SG confirms it before the dice hit the table. A character forcing open a door uses Might. A character picking a lock uses Reflex. A character enduring a hard winter march uses Endurance. The same goal can sometimes be approached through different attributes — the attribute you choose shapes both the roll and the fiction that follows.

Part Three covers how attributes are assigned at character creation and how they advance through play.

**_\[SIDEBAR: When no attribute is obvious\]_**

_Pick the attribute that best describes the approach you're taking — not the outcome you want. The SG will confirm or redirect before the roll. If two attributes both seem to fit, the player names one and explains how their character is approaching the task. That explanation does the work._

_\[END SIDEBAR\]_

### Skill Tiers

Rather than a long list of specific skills, the Willowlight Engine uses three tiers of expertise. At character creation you choose one Vocation, three Interests, and three Hobbies. These define what your character is good at and how good they are at it.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tier** | **Bonus** | **Scope** | **Rule** |
| Vocation | +3 (or higher) | Very broad — defines who you are | 1 Vocation per character |
| Interest | +2  | Moderately broad — an area of competence | 3 Interests at creation |
| Hobby | +1  | Narrow and specific | 3 Hobbies at creation |

Use the highest applicable tier bonus. Tiers do not stack. If your Vocation and an Interest both cover what you're doing, use the Vocation bonus and nothing more. Overlap is wasted.

When nothing applies, roll 3d6 + attribute alone. The attribute is always there as a fallback.

Vocations can deepen through play, advancing beyond +3 at significant cost and with narrative justification. Full advancement rules are in Part Three.

**EXAMPLE — Skill tiers in action**

_Elira's Vocation is Scholar. Her Interests are Investigation, Runelore, and Medicine. Her Hobbies include Awareness and Rhetoric._

_When she examines strange runes carved into a dungeon wall, her Vocation applies — scholars investigate such things. She rolls 3d6 + Reason + 3._

_When she tries to notice someone following the party through a market, her Hobby: Awareness applies. She rolls 3d6 + Guile + 1._

_When she attempts to pick a lock, nothing applies. She rolls 3d6 + Reflex alone._

### The Target Number

The SG sets a Target Number before you roll based on how difficult the task is. The TN ladder has six steps. Most tasks in ordinary play fall between TN 8 and TN 14.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Difficulty** | **TN** | **What it means** |
| Easy | 8   | Within reach for a competent person. Stakes are real even if the skill is common. |
| Moderate | 10  | A genuine challenge requiring attention and competence. |
| Challenging | 12  | Requires real skill or the right approach. Failure is likely for the unprepared. |
| Hard | 14  | At the edge of what's possible for a capable person. |
| Severe | 16  | Exceptional difficulty. Reserve for critical, high-stakes moments. |
| Legendary | 18  | Near the limit of mortal capability. Rare even in experienced play. |

Setting the TN before the roll is the SG's most important tool. If the fiction makes an action harder — an injured sword arm, a target who saw through your last bluff, an audience watching every move — set the TN to reflect that before dice touch the table. Adjusting after the roll undermines the player's ability to make informed choices.

### Tier Outcomes

Success and failure aren't binary. There are three possible outcomes depending on where your result falls relative to the TN.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Result** | **Outcome** | **What happens** |
| Meet the TN | Clean success | You accomplish what you set out to do. No cost, no complication. |
| One tier below TN (TN–2) | Success at cost | You get what you wanted, but something goes wrong alongside it. The SG introduces a complication as part of the success. |
| One tier above TN (TN+2) | Success plus | You accomplish your goal and something goes better than expected. |

Falling more than one tier below the TN is a failure — you don't get what you wanted, and the SG introduces a consequence. The difference between a near-failure and a bad failure is the SG's judgment call based on the fiction.

A success at cost is not a failure wearing a success mask. The player gets what they said they wanted. The cost is real, but it does not undo the accomplishment. If a character picks a lock at cost, the door is open — but they made noise, or the pick broke, or someone was watching.

**EXAMPLE — Tier outcomes**

_Royce tries to force open a jammed gate. TN 12. He rolls 3d6 + Might (3) + Soldier (3) = 3d6+6._

_He rolls a 6 — total 12. Clean success. The gate opens._

_He rolls a 4 — total 10. One tier below. Success at cost. The gate opens, but the mechanism is broken — it won't close again._

_He rolls an 8 — total 14. One tier above. Success plus. The gate opens easily and he spots a second entrance he hadn't noticed._

## THE LUCK DIE

_\[ART: A single distinct die rolling to a stop among two standard dice — moment of decision\]_

The Luck Die is the third die in every roll. It sits apart from the Result Dice before the roll. After the dice land, you can see all three. The Result Dice determine your total — but the Luck Die is an opportunity.

Luck tokens are a player resource. Every character starts each session with tokens equal to their Luck rating. Most characters begin with a rating of 1.

### Swapping the Luck Die

After rolling, if you have a Luck token, you may spend it to swap one of your Result Dice with the Luck Die. You choose which Result Die to replace. The swap is permanent — you can't swap back.

This is an informed decision. You see the Luck Die before choosing whether to spend. If the Luck Die shows a 6 and one of your Result Dice shows a 1, spending a token to make that swap is obvious. If the Luck Die shows a 2, you might decide the swap isn't worth it.

**_\[SIDEBAR: Two uses of the Luck Die\]_**

_Offensive: you see a high Luck Die and swap it in for a better total._

_Defensive: you see a low Luck Die and spend your token preemptively — because if you don't, the SG can force that swap and make your roll worse._

_\[END SIDEBAR\]_

### The SG and Taint

If you choose not to swap — or don't have a token to spend — the SG may spend 1 Taint to force the swap instead. The SG takes whichever die is worse for you. This is the adversarial edge of the mechanic: the Luck Die is always in play, always visible, always potentially dangerous.

Taint is the SG's resource pool — a pressure currency that builds when the characters make costly choices and is spent to push back. The full Taint system is in Part Five. For now, know that it exists and that the Luck Die is one of its primary tools.

### Earning Luck

You earn Luck tokens during play by hitting your character's milestones — the specific triggers tied to your Conviction and Path. When a milestone fires, you gain 1 Luck token immediately, regardless of how many you currently have. Milestones are covered fully in Part Two.

At session end, all tokens disappear. Your Luck pool resets to your Luck rating at the start of the next session. Tokens earned during a session are that session's resource — spend them or lose them.

### Complications

When the two Result Dice show a combined total of 2 or 3, a complication triggers — regardless of whether the overall roll succeeds or fails. A complication is something that goes wrong alongside the main outcome. It cannot be cancelled by succeeding.

The SG introduces the complication based on the fiction. "You pick the lock. It opens. But you heard footsteps on the stairs just as the tumblers clicked." The success stands. The complication is additional.

**_\[SIDEBAR: A note on the numbers\]_**

_A total of 2 or 3 on two d6 happens about 8% of the time — roughly one roll in twelve. Frequent enough to feel present at the table, rare enough to feel significant when it arrives._

_\[END SIDEBAR\]_

### Critical Success

When both Result Dice show 6, you score a critical success. The result automatically upgrades one tier: a success becomes a success plus, a success plus becomes an exceptional success with the SG describing something going even better than that.

Characters with relevant Aspects may choose a domain-specific critical effect instead of the tier upgrade. These are covered in Parts Three and Four. At the start of play, the tier upgrade is all you need to know.

## THE THREE DOMAINS

Every conflict in the Willowlight Engine targets one of three tracks. The Physical domain threatens the Vitality track. The Mental domain threatens the Willpower track. The Social domain threatens the Composure track.

What matters is not how something is done — not the weapon in hand or the words being spoken — but what is at stake. The domain is defined by the track being threatened. A poisoned blade threatens Vitality. A psychic intrusion threatens Willpower. A public accusation threatens Composure. The SG determines which track is at stake before the roll. If it's not obvious, the player states their intent and the SG decides.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain** | **Track** | **What depletes it** | **Full track means...** |
| Physical | Vitality | Wounds, poison, exhaustion, physical harm | Felled — physically incapacitated |
| Mental | Willpower | Argument, doubt, psychological pressure, cognitive assault | Broken — conviction shattered |
| Social | Composure | Reputation damage, public humiliation, social isolation | Fallen — standing destroyed |

### Health Tracks

Your three health tracks are derived from the domain-anchor attribute in each domain. Vitality is 3 + Endurance. Willpower is 3 + Resolve. Composure is 3 + Command. A character with Endurance 2 has 5 Vitality boxes.

As damage fills boxes, you move through three tiers of impairment. Penalties from all three tracks stack — a character under pressure in multiple domains is paying for it everywhere.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tier** | **Physical / Mental / Social name** | **When** | **Penalty** |
| 1   | Injured / Shaken / Rattled | Half empty | –1 to all rolls |
| 2   | Wounded / Strained / Exposed | 1–2 boxes left | –2 to all rolls |
| 3 — Break | Felled / Broken / Fallen | Zero boxes | –3 to all rolls, 1 action per round |

Breaking is not death. It is a threshold — a significant story moment that creates consequences, permanent or temporary, based on how it's handled. The specific consequences are covered in Part Five.

### Recovery

Health tracks recover between scenes, not between actions.

**Tier 1 damage** clears with a few minutes of rest at the end of a scene. No roll required.

**Tier 2 damage** requires a full scene of downtime and attention — bandaging, sleep, or appropriate care.

**Tier 3 damage (Break)** requires significant recovery time and, in some cases, specific narrative resolution. A Broken mental track may not recover until the character addresses what broke it.

## CONFLICT

When a situation escalates into structured opposition — a fight, a negotiation, an argument that matters — the table moves into conflict. Conflict is organized into rounds. Each character acts once per round. On your turn you have 3 actions.

The repeat penalty applies when you use the same action type more than once in a round: the second use costs –2 to your roll, the third costs –4. Heavy weapons impose –3 and –6 instead.

NPCs never roll dice. When an NPC acts against a player, the player rolls their defense. When a player attacks an NPC, the player rolls against the NPC's defense Target Number. The SG controls the fiction and the NPCs — the dice are always the players' tools.

### Physical Conflict

_\[ART: Dynamic combat scene — armored figure with weapon against two opponents in a torchlit corridor\]_

Physical conflict resolves violence and physical contests. It targets the Vitality track. Key actions:

**Attack:** Roll Might or Reflex + highest applicable skill vs. the target's Physical defense TN. On success, deal base weapon damage plus any tier points spent. Weapon rating sets base damage: Rating 1–2 weapons deal 1, Rating 3–4 deal 2, Rating 5 deals 3.

**Move:** Move to an adjacent range band. Free if uncontested; contested movement may require a roll.

**Defend:** Raise your Physical defense TN by 2 until your next turn.

**Assess:** Ask the SG one honest question about the scene, the enemy, or the environment.

**Aid:** Support an ally. They gain +1 on their next roll this round.

**Recover:** Clear one Tier 1 condition if you have a genuine moment of respite.

**EXAMPLE — Physical conflict**

_Royce faces four soldiers. TN 12, Threshold 2, Size 4. He attacks: 3d6 + Might (3) + Soldier (3). He rolls 9 — total 15. Two tiers above TN, 2 tier points._

_He deals 2 base damage (longsword, Rating 3) + 2 from tier point spends = 4 damage. Threshold is 2, so he drops two soldiers. Group TN drops to 10._

_Next turn he attacks again (–2 repeat penalty): 3d6+4. He rolls 8 — total 12. Still above TN 10. Another success._

### Social Conflict

Social conflict targets the Composure track. It resolves negotiations, confrontations, public debates — any situation where reputation and social standing are at stake.

The key tool is **leverage** — concrete information, evidence, or obligations you hold over the other party. Leverage adds to your roll when deployed and is always specific to a target or faction. It is never abstract.

Social conflict ends in concession, not elimination. The winner sets the broad outcome. The loser chooses its specific form — which concession they make, how they back down. This preserves player agency over their character's story even in defeat.

**Attack (social):** Roll Influence or Poise + highest applicable skill vs. the target's Social defense TN. On success, deal Composure damage plus tier points spent.

**Deploy Leverage:** Spend leverage (Minor +1, Major +2, Devastating +3) to boost your next roll. Leverage is consumed on use.

**Assess:** Ask the SG one honest question about the target's position, motivations, or vulnerabilities.

**Set Terms:** Declare the stakes before conflict begins. If you win, the terms hold.

**EXAMPLE — Social conflict**

_Mira is negotiating with Mayor Fenn, Social Scale 6, Social defense TN 14. She has leverage from information gathered earlier — she knows the town meeting vote was closer than Fenn has admitted._

_She deploys the leverage (+2) and attacks: 3d6 + Influence (3) + Death Priestess (3) + leverage (2) = 3d6+8 vs. TN 14. She rolls 8 — total 16. Two tiers above TN. Two tier points._

_She spends both: Fenn agrees to address the valley threat AND concedes that the town's support for the party is his stated position. He doesn't mean it. But now he's said it publicly._

### Mental Conflict

Mental conflict targets the Willpower track. It is the domain of argument, investigation, and psychological pressure. Mental conflict rewards preparation — knowing what a target believes and fears before pressing them is worth more than any raw bonus.

**Attack (mental):** Roll Reason or Guile + highest applicable skill vs. the target's Mental defense TN. On success, deal Willpower damage plus tier points spent.

**Assess:** Ask the SG one honest question about what the target believes, fears, or knows.

**Analyze:** Identify a weakness in the target's position. Reduces their Mental defense TN by 1 until end of scene. Succeeds automatically — no roll required.

**EXAMPLE — Mental conflict**

_Elira is pressing a forger about a disputed lineage document. The forger's Mental defense TN is 12._

_She first Assesses: 'What does this person most fear being exposed?' The SG tells her: the forger's own name appears in the archive records, implicating him in the original fraud._

_She Analyzes: 'The watermark on this page didn't exist until six years after this document was supposedly written.' The Analyze succeeds automatically. TN drops to 11._

_Now she attacks: 3d6 + Reason (3) + Scholar (3) = 3d6+6 vs. TN 11. She rolls 7 — total 13. Clean success. The forger's Willpower takes 1 damage. He admits the document was altered but claims he didn't know the full scope._

### Concession

Any character can concede at any point in a conflict. This is not weakness — it is a narrative tool. The winner sets the broad outcome. The loser chooses its specific form. Conceding voluntarily before a track breaks carries no mechanical consequences beyond the concession itself.

No one should feel their only option is to fight to zero. The fiction is always richer when characters make difficult choices before the math forces them to.

## WHAT KIND OF WORLD IS THIS?

The Willowlight Engine is designed to run stories in worlds where the past is not finished and the present costs something. Worlds where power has a face you can name, where old debts still bind, where the land has long memory and the dead do not always stay quiet.

The examples throughout this book draw from one such world — Wrath & Woe, a dark fantasy setting where communities struggle to hold together under pressures old and new. You will encounter characters trying to rebuild something worth having, a town with factions whose interests don’t align, and threats that move whether or not anyone is watching.

The engine is not a single world. Mount Ogden Gaming Company publishes setting supplements that run on the same rules. Drakorath is a hearthlight gothic political fantasy set in a world scarred by primordial invasion, where mortals, cinematic heroes, and apex dragon lords govern the realm in uneasy interdependence. Ghost Code is a post-apocalyptic transhumanist setting where mythic Sovereigns wield root-level authority over a reality being systematically converted from biological to digital. Same engine, different worlds, different stakes.

The rules work for any of these worlds and for any you build yourself. The examples in this book are one answer to what those rules look like in play. Use them as written, or let them suggest your own.

## THE IDENTITY GRID

_\[ART: Two overlapping circles — one labeled with Conviction words, one with Path words — intersection glowing\]_

Every character is defined by two identity choices: a Conviction (what you believe) and a Path (how you act on that belief).

Together they form the character's identity — the engine that generates milestones, defines what threatens you, shapes how Corruption accumulates, and determines how you break under pressure.

This is not a personality quiz. A Conviction is not a backstory element or a flavor note. It is a mechanical load-bearing structure. The Conviction you choose determines your Corruption source, your collapse mode, and four of your eight milestone triggers. The Path you choose determines how you recover, how your Corruption manifests, and the other four triggers.

Read through the Convictions and Paths before you choose. The combination that produces the character you want to play is the right one. Trust that instinct.

### Convictions

Your Conviction is the core belief that drives you. It defines:

**The Hearth You Defend** — What you protect. The specific thing your belief is organized around. The anchor that, when threatened, generates Corruption.

**The Dark That Presses In** — What threatens your Conviction. The specific kind of darkness that makes your Corruption grow.

**Collapse** — What happens to your Conviction when your Willpower track Breaks. The specific way your belief warps under extreme pressure.

**Personality** — A brief sketch of the traits this Conviction tends to produce. A starting point, not a constraint.

**Moral Dilemma** — The hard question your Conviction never fully resolves. The tension that makes playing this character interesting.

**Milestones (4)** — Specific triggers tied to acting on your belief. Hitting a milestone during play earns XP (and an immediate Luck token).

### Paths

Your Path is the orientation through which you express your Conviction — how you relate to the world around you. It defines:

**Path Collapse** — What happens to your Path when Corruption fires a Break. The specific way your method warps under darkness.

**Hope Recovery** — How you reduce Corruption. The specific activities that restore your grounding.

**Milestones (4)** — Specific triggers tied to your orientation. The other half of your advancement engine.

### Two Collapse Modes

Every character has two ways to break, each triggered by different mechanical conditions.

**Conviction Collapse** triggers when your Willpower track Breaks (hits zero). This is exhaustion — you've been pushed past your limit and your core belief warps under the pressure. Your specific collapse is described in your Conviction entry.

**Path Collapse** triggers as a Shattered Hearth consequence option when Corruption fires at Break. This is corruption — the world's darkness has gotten inside your method and twisted it. Your specific collapse is described in your Path entry.

_Path Collapse is a consequence option, not an automatic outcome. When Shattered Hearth fires, the player chooses from the severity-tiered consequence menu. Path Collapse is one of the options at the Significant and Major tiers — it should be chosen because it feels right for the character and the moment, not because it's the only available option._

### Milestones and XP

Each character has 8 milestone triggers: 4 from their Conviction and 4 from their Path. When a milestone triggers during play, two things happen immediately: you earn 1 XP, and you earn 1 Luck token. Maximum 3 XP from all sources per session (arc completion bonuses are separate). Maximum 2 XP from milestones per session — the third milestone in a session still earns a Luck token, just no additional XP.

Milestones are not objectives to optimize. They are a description of what this character finds meaningful. A character with Justice's milestones will naturally seek out situations involving accountability and protection. A character with Ruin's milestones will naturally work toward endings. The milestones make the character's motivation mechanically present at the table.

## CONVICTIONS

#### Ambition

**The Hearth You Defend** The position, influence, and control you have built. Hope returns when you advance, when your authority is recognized.

**The Dark That Presses In** Losing ground. Being defied, undermined, outmaneuvered, or made small. Despair rises when power slips.

**Collapse** You overreach — grasp harder, consolidate faster, make the ruthless move you'd been holding back, caring less about what it costs.

**_Personality_** _Calculating, disciplined, perceptive, patient, capable of genuine loyalty to those who prove worthy._

**Moral Dilemma** At what point does the accumulation of power stop being a means to an end and become the only end you have left?

**Milestones**

• Seize an Opportunity Others Missed: Act on an opening for advancement that required nerve, timing, or ruthlessness.

• Make the Hard Call: Make a decision that consolidates your power at genuine personal cost.

• Recognize the Price: Confront what your ambition has cost someone and sit with it honestly.

• Prove Your Authority Means Something: Use accumulated power to do something beyond your own advancement.

#### Balance

**The Hearth You Defend** The distribution of power, the checks that keep any single force from consuming everything else. Hope returns when balance is restored.

**The Dark That Presses In** Dominance — anything that grows so large it crowds out everything else. Despair rises when a single force becomes unchallengeable.

**Collapse** You overcorrect — swing to an extreme in the name of restoring equilibrium, becoming the very imbalance you were trying to prevent.

**_Personality_** _Pragmatic, impartial, introspective, adaptable, occasionally mistaking detachment for wisdom._

**Moral Dilemma** When true balance requires you to support something you find repugnant because the alternative is worse — how do you live with that?

**Milestones**

• Restore Balance to an Unjust Situation: Act to correct an extreme imbalance, ensuring no single force dominates.

• Accept a Necessary Loss: Allow something valuable to fall because balance demands it.

• Refuse to Choose a Side: Maintain principled neutrality despite intense pressure to commit.

• Humble the Overconfident: Intervene to ensure those who believe themselves untouchable face consequences.

#### Destruction

**The Hearth You Defend** The possibility of something better on the other side of the burning. Hope returns when something that needed ending ends.

**The Dark That Presses In** Permanence — things that have made themselves unchallengeable. Despair rises when destruction fails or you can no longer see what you were clearing ground for.

**Collapse** The purpose drops away and only the burning remains — you destroy without vision, without direction, clearing ground for nothing.

**_Personality_** _Revolutionary, visionary, relentless, capable of genuine tenderness toward what comes after._

**Moral Dilemma** When you can't clearly see what comes after — are you a revolutionary or just a destroyer?

**Milestones**

• Tear Down What Cannot Be Reformed: Destroy an institution or structure, ensuring it cannot return.

• Sacrifice Stability for Change: Choose disruption over order, accepting short-term suffering.

• Burn Something You Loved: Destroy something from your own past, proving nothing is too sacred to end.

• Show Someone the Other Side: Help another person see why what they're defending deserves to fall.

#### Devotion

**The Hearth You Defend** The specific people, relationships, and bonds that give your life meaning. Hope returns when you protect them, when the bond holds against pressure.

**The Dark That Presses In** A world that asks you to choose between your bonds and everything else. Despair rises when those you love are threatened, lost, or harmed by your choices.

**Collapse** You close inward, cutting off anyone outside your innermost circle, becoming capable of anything to protect what remains.

**_Personality_** _Fierce, present, warm, stubborn, occasionally blind to anything outside the circle of those they love._

**Moral Dilemma** How much of yourself — and how much of the world outside your bonds — are you willing to sacrifice for the ones you love?

**Milestones**

• Hold the Line for Someone You Love: Endure hardship, danger, or loss specifically to protect a bond that matters.

• Choose the Bond Over the Cause: Prioritize a specific person over a larger obligation, accepting the consequences.

• Be Present When It Counts: Show up for someone who needed you, in a moment that cost you something.

• Reckon With the Cost of Love: Confront the harm your devotion has caused and sit with it honestly.

#### Fanaticism

**The Hearth You Defend** The cause, the doctrine, the truth as you understand it. Hope returns when the mission advances without compromise.

**The Dark That Presses In** Deviation, compromise, doubt — in others and in yourself. Despair rises when the cause falters or the institution proves corrupt.

**Collapse** You purge — turn the zealotry inward or outward with no remaining filter, eliminating doubt wherever you find it.

**_Personality_** _Zealous, unwavering, disciplined, charismatic, capable of extraordinary sacrifice and extraordinary cruelty in the same breath._

**Moral Dilemma** If the institution you serve has betrayed the truth you serve it for — what do you owe the institution versus the truth?

**Milestones**

• Sacrifice for the Cause: Place the mission above your own comfort, safety, or relationships.

• Purge What Strays: Confront deviation from the true path, in an institution, a companion, or yourself.

• Defy Authority for the Truth: Reject orders that conflict with what you know to be right.

• Bring Others to the Path: Inspire or lead someone into genuine commitment.

#### Freedom

**The Hearth You Defend** The ability of people to choose, to resist, to refuse. Hope returns when someone is freed or a system of control is disrupted.

**The Dark That Presses In** Control — systems, people, and ideologies that decide for others how they should live. Despair rises when oppression succeeds.

**Collapse** You become controlling — certain you know what liberation looks like and willing to impose it, becoming the thing you spent your life fighting.

**_Personality_** _Free-spirited, rebellious, courageous, compassionate, sometimes unable to distinguish liberating people from overriding their choices._

**Moral Dilemma** When the people you're liberating choose something you believe will re-enslave them — do you respect their choice or protect them from it?

**Milestones**

• Defy Oppression: Stand against a system or authority that controls others, at personal risk.

• Liberate Someone: Free a person from captivity or control they could not escape alone.

• Risk Yourself for Another's Choice: Absorb a consequence to protect someone else's right to decide.

• Disrupt the System: Break or subvert an institution that enforces control.

#### Harmony

**The Hearth You Defend** The fragile peace between people, the possibility of redemption, the belief that most harm can be prevented with patient work. Hope returns when conflict is avoided or suffering eased.

**The Dark That Presses In** A world that rewards force and punishes patience, where peace is treated as weakness. Despair rises when violence is the only answer left.

**Collapse** You go quiet — stop intervening, stop mediating, stop believing any of it matters. A withdrawal that looks like peace and is actually surrender.

**_Personality_** _Kind, compassionate, practical, diplomatic, occasionally too willing to absorb what others should be held responsible for._

**Moral Dilemma** How much harm do you accept in the short term to preserve the possibility of a peaceful outcome?

**Milestones**

• Prevent Conflict Without Force: Resolve a dispute through patience and skill rather than threat or combat.

• Show Mercy to a Fallen Foe: Grant someone a second chance when the easier choice was not to.

• Sacrifice for the Greater Good: Endure personal loss or hardship to preserve peace at a larger scale.

• Guide Another Toward Redemption: Help someone move away from harm and toward something better, without guarantees.

#### Justice

**The Hearth You Defend** The idea that wrongs can be made right, that accountability is possible, that the innocent deserve protection. Hope returns when justice is actually served.

**The Dark That Presses In** A world where the guilty walk free, where the law protects the powerful, where doing the right thing costs more than anyone should have to pay.

**Collapse** You become the thing you fought against — certain of your own righteousness, willing to impose your version of right without process, without mercy, without doubt.

**_Personality_** _Dutiful, honorable, disciplined, idealistic, occasionally inflexible._

**Moral Dilemma** When the law itself is the instrument of injustice, how far outside it are you willing to go?

**Milestones**

• Uphold Justice Over Law: Defy an unjust law or ruling to serve the justice it was supposed to represent, at genuine personal risk.

• Protect the Innocent at Personal Cost: Place yourself between someone and harm, knowing it will cost you.

• Deliver Righteous Judgment: Hold someone accountable through fair means, leaving room for mercy without abandoning accountability.

• Struggle With the Cost: Face a choice where justice and mercy conflict and accept the consequences of what you decide.

#### Nihilism

**The Hearth You Defend** Honesty — the refusal to comfort yourself with lies about meaning. Hope returns from small, concrete things: a job done well, a genuine connection, surviving another day.

**The Dark That Presses In** The weight of a world that keeps asking you to care about things that don't hold up under scrutiny. Despair rises when even the small things stop being sufficient.

**Collapse** The last small things stop being enough — you become genuinely hollow, acting without purpose, present without being there.

**_Personality_** _Deceptive, opportunistic, pragmatic, occasionally and unexpectedly kind in ways they immediately try to explain away._

**Moral Dilemma** If nothing matters — why does it still feel like some things do?

**Milestones**

• Act Anyway: Do something useful or decent despite having no belief that it matters.

• Exploit an Opportunity: Take what's available without apology, honest about what you're doing.

• Name the Emptiness: Confront the absence of meaning directly, without flinching or filling it with false comfort.

• Find the Thing That Still Holds: Discover something small that matters to you despite everything.

#### Rootless

**The Hearth You Defend** Moments of provisional belonging — the campfire that lasts one night, the companion who travels the same road for a while. Hope returns from connection that doesn't demand you stay.

**The Dark That Presses In** Being pushed out again. Despair rises when belonging is denied, when you reach for connection and it pulls back, or when you leave first because waiting for it to collapse is unbearable.

**Collapse** You leave — people, places, causes — before anything can push you out again. You burn the bridge yourself because waiting for it to collapse is unbearable.

**_Personality_** _Self-sufficient, observant, quietly longing, uncomfortable with need._

**Moral Dilemma** If you let yourself belong somewhere, you give the world something else to take from you. Is that a risk worth taking?

**Milestones**

• Stay When Every Instinct Says Leave: Remain with people despite the pull to go.

• Accept Provisional Belonging: Let yourself be part of something temporary without undermining it.

• Name What You Lost: Acknowledge a specific home, person, or place the world took from you.

• Let Someone In: Allow another person to know something real about you.

#### Selfish

**The Hearth You Defend** Yourself — your comfort, your freedom, your options. Hope returns when you get what you want, when you come out ahead.

**The Dark That Presses In** Obligation — the endless demands other people make on your time, loyalty, and resources. Despair rises when you're trapped by circumstances you can't maneuver out of.

**Collapse** You burn everything — relationships, opportunities, your own reputation — grabbing what you can before the situation collapses.

**_Personality_** _Charming, impulsive, adaptable, occasionally startled by their own capacity for something that looks uncomfortably like loyalty._

**Moral Dilemma** When getting what you want requires becoming someone you might not be able to live with — is there a line?

**Milestones**

• Take What You Want: Act in your own interest, acquiring something of genuine value.

• Walk Away From an Obligation: Leave a commitment behind when it no longer serves you.

• Manipulate Someone Effectively: Get someone to act in your interest through charm, deception, or leverage.

• Find the Line: Encounter something you won't do even for significant personal gain.

#### Survival

**The Hearth You Defend** Your life and the lives of whoever you've decided you can't afford to lose. Hope returns when you make it through, when another day happens.

**The Dark That Presses In** A world that is trying to kill you and everyone you care about. Despair rises when staying alive costs you something you can't get back.

**Collapse** You do something you swore you'd never do — cross the last line that defined who you were — and the worst part is it works.

**_Personality_** _Resourceful, unsentimental, fiercely competent, capable of warmth that surprises people who only see the hard edges._

**Moral Dilemma** When surviving requires becoming someone the person you were protecting would no longer recognize — is that survival or a different kind of death?

**Milestones**

• Get Through It: Survive something that should have killed you through competence or sheer refusal to stop.

• Keep Someone Else Alive: Get another person through something they couldn't have managed alone.

• Cross the Line: Do something you said you'd never do because the alternative was worse.

• Remember What You're Surviving For: Reconnect with something beyond survival itself and let it matter.

## THE EIGHT PATHS

Your Path is how you act on your Conviction — your orientation in the world. Where your Conviction defines what you believe, your Path defines how you engage with everything around that belief.

The four orientations describe your fundamental relationship to the world. For Paths protect and serve. Alongside Paths bond and direct. Apart Paths endure and observe. Against Paths resist and destroy. A character's Conviction might be the same as another's, but a different Path produces an entirely different way of living it out.

### Covenant

**Path Collapse** You enforce your bonds past the point of health — holding people to commitments that no longer serve them, turning love into obligation and loyalty into debt.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when a bond holds under pressure — when someone shows up, when trust proves warranted.

**Milestones**

• Make a binding commitment to another person.

• Honor a covenant when breaking it would be easier.

• Repair a bond that was damaged.

• Bring two parties into agreement or mutual trust.

### Defiance

**Path Collapse** You start fighting everything — not just what deserves it — picking battles that can't be won and burning resources on resistance that serves no one.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when defiance works — when refusal costs the person who expected compliance, when the pushback lands.

**Milestones**

• Refuse a demand from someone with power over you.

• Stand against something knowing you might lose.

• Protect someone's ability to choose by absorbing the consequence yourself.

• Turn an attempt to control you into a demonstration of its limits.

### Leadership

**Path Collapse** You stop trusting anyone else's judgment, stop sharing the weight, make every decision alone and call it protection — until the loneliness becomes its own kind of breaking.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when the people you lead succeed, when a decision proves right, when someone follows you into something hard and comes out the other side.

**Milestones**

• Make a decision that others follow at personal risk to yourself.

• Inspire someone to act beyond what they thought possible.

• Bear responsibility for an outcome your choices created.

• Unite fractured people around a common purpose.

### Ruin

**Path Collapse** You start destroying things that didn't need destroying — relationships, opportunities, parts of yourself — unable to distinguish what needs to end from what you're afraid to keep.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when destruction serves its purpose — when what needed ending ends, when the clearing makes space for something worth having.

**Milestones**

• Destroy something that needed destroying even at personal cost.

• Sacrifice something you built or valued for a greater purpose.

• Make the irrevocable choice when a reversible one was available.

• Clear the ground so something new can grow.

### Service

**Path Collapse** You lose the ability to distinguish your own needs from the needs of what you serve — you give until there is nothing left and call it virtue.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when your service produces a tangible result — someone helped, something accomplished, a need genuinely met.

**Milestones**

• Subordinate your own goal to someone else's need.

• Act on behalf of something larger without recognition or reward.

• Follow through on an obligation when breaking it would benefit you.

• Give something you cannot get back.

### Stewardship

**Path Collapse** You become controlling — suffocating, unable to let your charge face any risk. Your protection becomes a cage.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when something under your protection survives, recovers, or thrives because of your intervention.

**Milestones**

• Stand between someone and harm.

• Tend to something damaged until it recovers.

• Sacrifice a resource or advantage to preserve something for others.

• Refuse to abandon a charge even when it costs you.

### Vigil

**Path Collapse** You wait past the point of usefulness — watching when you should act, enduring what should be refused, mistaking inaction for discipline.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when endurance proves meaningful — when waiting was right, when the thing you watched over survived because you stayed.

**Milestones**

• Endure a prolonged hardship without breaking.

• Observe and act on something others missed by moving too fast.

• Hold a position or commitment alone without support.

• Wait when action would feel better but cost more.

### Wandering

**Path Collapse** You run from something that needed facing — the running solves nothing but feels like the only option left.

**Hope Recovery** Corruption reduces when movement produces something — a discovery, a connection made in passing, a problem solved by arriving somewhere new.

**Milestones**

• Move on from something before it can root you.

• Find what you need in an unfamiliar place.

• Survive through adaptability rather than preparation.

• Offer something genuine to people you won't stay with.

## ATTRIBUTES

_\[ART: Character sheet showing the nine attribute boxes organized by domain, some filled in\]_

Every character has nine attributes organized across three domains. Attributes are rated 1 to 5. All nine start at 1 at creation.

At character creation, assign a domain priority to each of your three domains: one becomes your Primary domain, one your Secondary, and one your Tertiary. Then distribute bonus points within each domain:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain priority** | **Bonus points to distribute within that domain** | **Creation ceiling** |
| Primary | 4 bonus points — distribute freely across the three attributes in this domain | 3   |
| Secondary | 2 bonus points — distribute freely across the three attributes in this domain | 3   |
| Tertiary | 1 bonus point — assign to any one attribute in this domain | 3   |

No attribute may exceed 3 at creation. Ratings of 4 and 5 are earned through play. Bonus points that would push an attribute past 3 can be redirected to another attribute in the same domain.

### The Nine Attributes

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain** | **Attribute** | **What it governs** | **Domain anchor function** |
| Physical | Might | Force, raw power, physical imposition — the fallback for physical attacks when no skill applies |     |
| Physical | Reflex | Speed, precision, agility — governs Physical initiative |     |
| Physical | Endurance | Resilience, staying power — fallback for physical endurance | Vitality track size (3 + Endurance) and Physical defense TN (8 + Endurance) |
| Mental | Reason | Analysis, knowledge, intellectual force — fallback for mental attacks when no skill applies |     |
| Mental | Guile | Deception, cunning, misdirection — governs Mental initiative |     |
| Mental | Resolve | Mental fortitude, conviction — fallback for mental endurance | Willpower track size (3 + Resolve) and Mental defense TN (8 + Resolve) |
| Social | Influence | Presence, persuasion, social force — fallback for social attacks when no skill applies |     |
| Social | Poise | Grace, adaptability, social finesse — governs Social initiative |     |
| Social | Command | Authority, coordination — fallback for social endurance | Composure track size (3 + Command) and Social defense TN (8 + Command) |

_Domain anchors: Endurance, Resolve, and Command are the mechanical anchors of their domains. They determine how much damage you can absorb and how hard you are to affect. Characters who invest heavily in their primary domain's anchor attribute are more durable in that domain — but potentially fragile in the others._

**EXAMPLE — Attribute assignment**

_Royce is a Physical Primary character. Physical domain gets 4 bonus points. He puts 2 into Might (now 3), 1 into Reflex (now 2), and 1 into Endurance (now 2). No attribute exceeds the creation ceiling of 3._

_His Secondary domain is Mental. 2 bonus points. He puts 1 into Guile (now 2) and 1 into Resolve (now 2). Reason stays at 1 — he's not a scholar._

_His Tertiary domain is Social. 1 bonus point. He puts it into Command (now 2). Influence and Poise stay at 1._

_Result: Might 3, Reflex 2, Endurance 2 / Reason 1, Guile 2, Resolve 2 / Influence 1, Poise 1, Command 2._

_Derived stats: Vitality 5 (3+2), Willpower 5 (3+2), Composure 5 (3+2). Physical defense TN 10 (8+2), Mental TN 10 (8+2), Social TN 10 (8+2)._

### Attribute Advancement

Attributes increase through XP spend. Cost scales by the attribute's domain priority:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain priority** | **Cost per dot** | **Example** |
| Primary | New rating × 2 XP | Raising Might from 3 to 4 costs 8 XP (primary domain) |
| Secondary | New rating × 3 XP | Raising Resolve from 2 to 3 costs 9 XP (secondary domain) |
| Tertiary | New rating × 5 XP | Raising Influence from 1 to 2 costs 10 XP (tertiary domain) |

Priority is fixed at creation but can shift at major milestones when the fiction supports it — a character whose story has moved them into a fundamentally different way of engaging with the world may renegotiate their domain priority with SG approval. Cost tier changes going forward only; previous purchases are unaffected.

The maximum attribute rating is 5. There is no XP cost that automatically unlocks 4 or 5 — the narrative gate applies. A rating of 4 requires the character to have genuinely grown into that level of capability in the fiction. A rating of 5 is exceptional by any standard.

## SKILLS

Skills are organized into three tiers: one Vocation, three Interests, and three Hobbies. Use the highest applicable tier for any roll. Tiers do not stack.

### Choosing Your Vocation

Your Vocation is who you are in the world — the broadest and most defining description of your expertise. It is two words at most. It implies a lifestyle, a history, and a set of competencies that belong together.

Good Vocations are broad enough to cover a range of related activities but specific enough to exclude unrelated ones. The SG decides whether a Vocation applies in a given situation. A player who argues for a generous interpretation should expect to be asked how their character's specific background prepares them for this moment.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vocation** | **What it covers** | **What it doesn't cover** |
| Combat Veteran | Combat, tactics, physical training, command under pressure, reading threats | Scholarly research, social maneuvering, arcane knowledge |
| Merchant Envoy | Negotiation, trade routes, reading people, managing deals, commercial knowledge | Physical labor, combat, arcane or scholarly pursuits |
| Scholar | Research, analysis, academic knowledge, investigation across domains | Physical confrontation, practical crafts, social maneuvering |
| Death Priest | Spiritual care, ritual knowledge, comfort of the dying, dealing with the dead | Combat, scholarly research, political maneuvering |
| Wardwright | Protective magic, warding traditions, identifying magical threats | Offensive magic, non-magical combat, social politics |
| Ranger | Wilderness survival, tracking, archery, stealth in natural settings | Urban stealth, scholarly research, formal social contexts |
| Skald | Oral tradition, performance, cultural memory, moving between social registers | Technical crafts, arcane scholarship, physical labor |
| Physician | Medicine, anatomy, diagnosis, stabilizing wounds, disease management | Combat, social politics, arcane knowledge |

Vocations can advance beyond their starting bonus through XP and narrative justification. See the Advancement section for costs.

### Choosing Your Interests

Interests are moderately broad areas of competence — things you've studied, practiced, or developed real skill in. They are narrower than your Vocation but broader than your Hobbies. You have three at creation.

Interests should not overlap with your Vocation. If you are a Combat Veteran, taking an Interest in Tactics is wasted — your Vocation already covers it at a higher bonus. Use your Interests to fill gaps and expand into adjacent territory.

**EXAMPLE — Interests for a Combat Veteran**

_Useful: Persuasion (fills a social gap), Medicine (adjacent to military life), History (academic interest the Vocation doesn't cover), Sailing (practical skill from service)._

_Wasted: Combat, Physical Training, Tactics — the Vocation already covers these at +3._

### Choosing Your Hobbies

Hobbies are narrow, specific competencies — things your character does well but only in a defined context. They contribute +1 to a roll and apply only when the situation specifically calls for that exact thing.

Good Hobbies are specific. Lockpicking is a Hobby. Stealth is probably Interest or Vocation territory. Card games is a Hobby. Gambling is an Interest.

### Advancement

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purchase** | **Cost** | **Cap** |
| New Hobby | 3 XP | 5 Hobbies total (creation + advancement) |
| New Interest | 5 XP | 5 Interests total (creation + advancement) |
| Vocation +4 | 10 XP | Narrative gate — high bar |
| Vocation +5 | 20 XP | Narrative gate — mastery |

Buying a new Interest or Hobby represents genuine broadening of expertise — not getting better at what you already do, but becoming competent in a new domain. Depth within a tier is expressed through Aspects and Edges.

Vocation advancement beyond +3 reflects genuine identity deepening — a soldier who has become a legendary commander, a scholar whose name is cited in other scholars' work. The narrative gate is genuinely high. The XP is necessary but not sufficient.

## EDGES

_\[ART: Character sheet close-up showing Edge dots filled in with deliberate care\]_

Edges are permanent beneficial traits that define specific capabilities beyond your skill tiers. They are rated 1 to 5 dots and purchased with XP through narrative justification. At character creation, distribute 7 Edge dots across any Edges you qualify for.

Edges are not abstract bonuses. Each Edge does something specific and named. The specificity is the point — an Edge should describe something real about who this character has become.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |

| Scaling | Rated 1–5. Each dot increases the benefit. Purchased one dot at a time. Cost: new rating × 2 XP. |

| Repeatable | Can be taken multiple times for different applications. 2 XP per purchase. |

### The Narrative Gate

XP is necessary but not sufficient for purchasing an Edge. The player must explain how the character earned it — either the fiction already supports it (hard justification) or a plan the SG approves is in motion (prospective justification). The group adjudicates sufficiency. Unspent XP is never lost.

_Worked example — hard justification: Elira has been researching the local archive for three sessions, spending downtime actions there. She wants to purchase Library Access. The fiction supports it; the SG approves immediately._

_Worked example — prospective justification: Royce wants Dual Strike ••. He has the Interest to qualify but the fiction hasn't shown him training for it. He tells the SG: I want to spend the next arc specifically working on this. The SG sets a milestone — defeat a skilled opponent using only the off-hand — and Royce pursues it._

Burdens can be reduced or removed through XP spend, but XP is not sufficient on its own. The narrative gate applies to each dot individually — the fiction must support removal at each step.

## STARTER CONTACTS AND WEALTH

### Starter Contacts

At character creation, every character names two starter contacts — specific people they know, trust to some degree, and can call on. These contacts are free. They do not cost Edge dots.

Each starter contact begins at connection health 2 (Warm). Name them specifically. Give each a relationship sentence — not just 'blacksmith in Goldenfields' but 'Corvin, the farmer who seconded the motion to give us the Dragon Flagon, because he thought we'd earned it and he respects people who earn things.'

Starter contacts can be anyone within reason for your character's background. They do not need to be powerful or well-connected. What matters is that they are real people with their own interests, who have a reason to help you and a reason to sometimes not.

_Connection health tracks: All contacts — starter contacts, Edge-purchased Contacts, and relationships built through play — use the same health track system. Health 3 = Strong (goes out of their way for you). Health 2 = Warm (solid, in good standing). Health 1 = Distant (cooled, guarded). Health 0 = Strained (reluctant, −1 on rolls relying on them). Full rules in Part Five._

# PART FOUR: ASPECTS

_\[ART: Character sheet section showing Aspect tree dots filled, progression visible\]_

Aspects are learned abilities that go beyond what skill and attribute alone can achieve. They represent training, talent, and the specific ways a character has developed their capabilities into something more than competent. Every Aspect does something precise and named.

Aspects are organized into four catalogs. Attribute Aspects are universal mechanical capability — any character may develop them. Conviction Aspects express what you believe in action. Path Aspects express how you act on that belief. General Aspects are standalone practical competencies with no tree structure.

Arcane Aspects represent learned command over forces outside the three core domains — setting supplements expand this catalog with tradition-specific trees.

## Cost Types

Every Aspect ability uses one or more of the following cost types. Many abilities combine costs.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Cost type** | **What it means** |
| Passive | Always active. No cost, no action, no roll. The ability simply is. |
| Action cost | Spend the listed number of actions from your turn to activate. The ability fires whether or not an associated roll succeeds, unless stated otherwise. |
| Tier point activation | The ability only activates if you spend the listed number of tier points after a successful roll. Tier points are only generated on a success — this means the ability cannot fire on a failure. |
| Enhancement | Spend additional tier points after a successful roll to improve what the ability does. Optional — you choose whether to spend. |
| Luck token | Some abilities list a Luck token as an alternative to a tier point spend. You choose which currency to use at the moment of activation. |
| Corruption | Automatic at dot 3–4 (1 Corruption) and dot 5 (2 Corruption). Fires on activation regardless of other costs. Not listed separately in ability entries — the dot level tells you the cost. |

_The +2 modifier cap: Passive modifier bonuses from all Aspect sources combined cannot exceed +2 per roll type. This applies to modifier bonuses only — tier point generation, conditions, and narrative effects are not subject to this cap._

_Sustained abilities: Some dot 4–5 abilities can be maintained across rounds. Activation cost is separate from sustain cost. Sustaining costs 1 action per round regardless of dot level._

# PART FIVE: CONFLICT

_The full conflict rules_

## WHAT IS THIS CONFLICT?

Conflict in the Willowlight Engine is organized around one question: what is this conflict trying to damage?

The answer determines the domain. Not how the conflict is being conducted — not the weapon in hand or the words being spoken — but what is at stake. The domain is always defined by the track being threatened.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Domain** | **What's at stake** |
| Physical | The body's capacity to function. Damage here means you are physically less able. Combat is one way to get there. Exhaustion, poison, a collapsing building are others. |
| Mental | Your internal certainty. Damage here means you can no longer hold your own position — you doubt, you fracture, you recant. Scholarly argument is one mode. Psychological pressure, deliberate deception, revelations that shatter a core belief are others. |
| Social | Your standing in relation to others. Damage here means the room, the faction, the world sees you differently. Persuasion is one tool. Public humiliation, exposure, and deliberate rumor are others. |

This distinction matters because it separates the mode of conflict from its domain. A poisoned blade, a psychic intrusion, and a brutal interrogation look completely different in the fiction. All three target Vitality. All three are Physical domain conflicts.

Physical domain is not 'fighting.' Social domain is not 'talking.' Mental domain is not 'knowing things.' Those are tools. The domain is always what's at stake.

Mental is the domain most easily misread. The instinct is to think of it as knowledge — who has better information. But the Mental domain is about belief and certainty. Knowledge is a weapon you bring to Mental conflict, not the conflict itself. The Willpower track breaks when someone can no longer hold their position — not because they ran out of facts, but because they can no longer sustain the certainty required to keep arguing.

### Mixed Domains

Parties in the same conflict choose their domain independently. One side may make a Mental assault on the other's Willpower while the other makes a Social attack on the first's Composure. Both are using the same resolution system. Both are generating damage. The tracks being targeted are simply different.

Additional characters can contribute by attacking different tracks than the lead. A party that coordinates domain choices — one pressing Mental, one pressing Social, one maintaining physical presence — imposes cumulative penalties that a single-domain approach cannot match.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Track impairment tier** | **Penalty to all rolls** |
| Tier 1 (half empty) | −1  |
| Tier 2 (1–2 boxes left) | −2  |
| Tier 3 / Break (zero boxes) | −3, 1 action per round |

Penalties from all three tracks stack. There is no cap. A character at Tier 3 on all three tracks is at −9, which the fiction has almost certainly resolved before the math gets there.

### The Cost of Domain Choice

Every domain carries consequences beyond the dice. Drawing a blade in a courtroom ends the legal proceeding and starts a criminal one. A social attack that destroys someone's reputation creates enemies who remember. A mental assault on someone who did nothing wrong is a violation — characters who do this accumulate Corruption even when they win. The engine does not restrict domain access. The fiction has opinions about it. The SG expresses those opinions through the TN before the roll.

## CONFLICT OVERVIEW

### Structure

Conflict is organized into rounds. Each character acts once per round in initiative order. On your turn you have 3 actions. Actions are declared and resolved one at a time. Conflict ends when one side concedes, all members of one side have Broken, or the fictional situation resolves without further mechanical resolution.

### Initiative

At the start of conflict, each character rolls their domain-appropriate initiative attribute against TN 8. The result determines the order.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **If the conflict is primarily...** | **Initiative attribute** |
| Physical | Reflex |
| Mental | Guile |
| Social | Poise |
| Mixed or unclear | Each character uses their highest initiative attribute |

Ties are broken by the full roll result. If still tied, the player character acts before the NPC.

### NPCs Never Roll

When an NPC acts against a player character, the player rolls their defense against the NPC's TN. When a player character attacks an NPC, the player rolls against the NPC's defense TN. The SG sets TNs and controls fiction. The players roll all dice.

_Knowing what NPCs want: Before conflict begins, know what each NPC is trying to accomplish. Not 'defeat the players' — that's not a goal a person has. A guard captain wants to protect the garrison and go home. An NPC who is losing does not keep fighting until their TN hits zero unless they have a strong reason to do so. Most people concede when they understand they've lost._

## THE ACTION ECONOMY

### Three Actions

Each character has 3 actions per turn. The repeat penalty applies when you use the same action type more than once in a round.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Use** | **Repeat penalty** |
| Second use of the same action type | −2 to the roll |
| Third use of the same action type | −4 to the roll |
| Heavy weapon: second use | −3 to the roll |
| Heavy weapon: third use | −6 to the roll |

Switching between attack types — melee to ranged, physical to social — does not reset the repeat penalty. All Attack actions share a repeat pool regardless of which weapon or skill you use.

### The Universal Actions

These actions are available in any conflict domain unless a specific rule states otherwise.

#### **Attack**

Roll an appropriate attribute + highest applicable skill vs. the target's defense TN. On success, deal base damage + tier points spent. In physical conflict, weapon rating sets base damage. In social and mental conflict, a successful attack deals 1 track damage + tier points spent.

#### **Move**

Move to an adjacent range band. Free if uncontested; contested movement may require a roll against TN 8 or the opponent's relevant defense TN.

#### **Defend**

Raise your domain-appropriate defense TN by 2 until the start of your next turn. You may only Defend in one domain per action.

#### **Assess**

Ask the SG one honest question about the scene, an opponent, the environment, or the current situation. The SG answers truthfully within what your character could plausibly observe or infer. Good Assess questions provide fictional information that changes how you approach the conflict.

#### **Aid**

Support an ally. They gain +1 on their next roll this round. No roll required. You may Aid in any domain.

#### **Recover**

Clear one Tier 1 condition if you have a genuine moment of respite. The SG determines whether the fiction supports a Recover action. You cannot Recover while under active attack.

#### **Grapple / Pin**

Attempt to impose the Restrained condition. Opposed roll: your Might vs. the target's Physical defense TN. On success, target is Restrained. A Restrained target can attempt to break free as one of their actions (same opposed roll).

#### **Disengage**

Safely exit a grapple or close engagement without provoking a free attack. Moving away without Disengaging allows opponents one free attack as you go.

## PHYSICAL CONFLICT

_\[ART: Royce against two Pale Vanguard soldiers — armored figure, longsword, torchlit corridor, controlled aggression\]_

Physical conflict resolves violence, physical contests, and any situation where the body's capacity to function is what's being threatened.

### Physical Actions

#### **Shield / Cover**

Use an action to take cover or raise a shield. Until your next turn, ranged attacks against you suffer a penalty equal to the cover's rating (light −1, medium −2, heavy −3). Moving out of cover ends the effect.

### Damage and Weapon Rating

Weapon rating sets base damage. It does not add to your attack roll.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weapon rating** | **Base damage** | **Examples** |
| 1–2 | 1   | Dagger, short sword, improvised weapon, unarmed |
| 3–4 | 2   | Longsword, spear, hand axe, short bow |
| 5   | 3   | Greatsword, greataxe, war hammer, longbow |

On a successful attack, deal base damage + tier points spent. Tier points spent on damage add 1 damage per point (cap 3 total from all sources).

### Weapon Tags

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tag** | **Type** | **Effect** |
| Brutal | Spend (1 tier point) | Deal +2 damage instead of the standard +1 from a tier point spend. |
| Piercing | Spend (1 tier point) | Ignore the armor contribution to the target's Physical defense TN on this attack. |
| Rapid Strike | Spend (1 tier point) | Light weapons only. Make an additional Attack this turn with no repeat penalty. Once per turn. |
| Reach | Always on | +1 range band on attacks. Opponents must close distance or suffer −1 to attack roll against you. |
| Versatile | Always on | +1 to attack roll when wielded two-handed. |
| Slow | Always on | The first attack with this weapon counts as two uses for the repeat penalty. |

### Weapon Weight and Repeat Penalty

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight** | **Repeat penalty** | **Examples** |
| Light | −2 / −4 | Dagger, short sword |
| Medium | −2 / −4 | Longsword, spear, short bow |
| Heavy | −3 / −6 | Greataxe, greatsword, war hammer |

### Critical Effects in Physical Conflict

When both Result Dice show 6, characters with relevant Physical Aspect dots may choose a domain-specific critical effect instead of the standard tier upgrade:

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Critical effect** | **Aspect requirement** |
| Devastating Strike — deal +3 damage instead of tier upgrade | Juggernaut •• or Blade •• |
| Staggering Critical — target is Prone and cannot act until they spend an action standing | Juggernaut ••• |
| Precision Critical — ignore all armor and Scale gap on this attack | Blade ••• |
| Enduring Critical — clear 1 Vitality box in addition to normal crit effect | Sentinel •• |

### Mook Groups

Minor enemies are handled as groups rather than individuals. A Mook Group is a single entity with one stat block.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stat** | **What it means** | **Typical range** |
| TN  | Covers all actions. Drops by 1 per member lost. | 8–12 |
| Threshold | Damage needed to drop one member. Hits below Threshold don't remove anyone. | 2–3 |
| Size | Starting group size. | 3–6 |
| Trait | Optional. Mechanically relevant traits get one sentence. | As needed |

Mook Groups rout when it makes narrative sense. A group of five guards doesn't fight to the last person against someone who has already dropped three of them.

**EXAMPLE — Mook Group in combat**

_Royce faces four Pale Vanguard soldiers. TN 12, Threshold 2, Size 4._

_He attacks: 3d6 + Might (3) + Combat Veteran (3) = 3d6+6 vs. TN 12. He rolls 9 — total 15. Two tiers above. 2 tier points._

_2 base damage (longsword, Rating 3) + 2 from tier points = 4 damage. Threshold 2: two soldiers drop. Group TN falls to 10._

_Next turn (−2 repeat): 3d6+4 vs. TN 10. He rolls 8 — total 12. One tier above. 1 tier point. Deals 3 damage. One more drops. Size 1, TN 9._

_The last soldier looks at Royce. Royce looks back. The SG rules the rout._

## SOCIAL CONFLICT

_\[ART: Mira across a table from Mayor Fenn — lantern between them, her stillness against his discomfort\]_

Social conflict targets the Composure track. It resolves negotiations, confrontations, and any situation where reputation and standing are what's at stake.

Social conflict ends in concession, not elimination. The winner sets the broad outcome. The loser chooses its specific form. This preserves player agency over their character's story even in defeat.

### Leverage

Leverage is concrete information, evidence, or obligations you hold over another party. It is never abstract. It is always specific to a target.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Leverage tier** | **Bonus when deployed** | **What it represents** |
| Minor (+1) | Spend for +1 to your next Social roll | Something awkward. A rumor. A small debt. A contradiction. |
| Major (+2) | Spend for +2 to your next Social roll | Something damaging. Evidence of wrongdoing. A significant obligation. |
| Devastating (+3) | Spend for +3 to your next Social roll | Something ruinous. Proof of a serious crime. Information that changes everything. |

Leverage is consumed when deployed. Gathering leverage through investigation, conversation, or downtime before a social conflict is often the most important preparation you can do.

### Social Actions

#### **Attack (Social)**

Roll Influence or Poise + highest applicable skill vs. the target's Social defense TN. On success, deal 1 Composure damage + tier points spent.

#### **Deploy Leverage**

Spend leverage to boost your next Social roll by its tier bonus. Declare before rolling. Leverage is consumed whether the roll succeeds or fails.

#### **Shift Venue**

Move the conversation to a context that favors your position. Roll Poise + relevant skill vs. TN 10. On success, the venue bonus shifts by +1 or −1 in your favor for the rest of the scene.

#### **Expose Weakness**

Identify a vulnerability in the target's position. Roll Reason + Investigation vs. TN 10. On success, the target's Social defense TN is reduced by 1 until end of scene. Requires a different weakness each time.

#### **Rally Support**

Appeal to bystanders. Roll Influence + relevant skill vs. TN 10 (modified by audience sympathy). On success, gain Minor leverage and the target suffers −1 to their next Social attack as the room shifts.

#### **Set Terms**

Declare the stakes before conflict continues. State specifically what you want if you win. If you win, those terms hold — they cannot renegotiate afterward.

#### **Two Truths**

Present three statements — two true, one false. Target guesses the lie. Wrong guess: 1 Composure damage and you generate Minor leverage. Right guess: you take 1 Composure damage.

### Critical Effects in Social Conflict

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Critical effect** | **Aspect requirement** |
| Devastating Credibility — target's Social defense TN permanently reduced by 1 for this arc | Sovereign •• or Iron Reputation Edge |
| Crowd Turns — all bystanders shift to your side; target gains Discredited condition | Sovereign ••• |
| Leverage Cascade — generate one leverage tier higher than your roll would normally produce | Diplomat •• or Silver Tongue Edge •• |
| Perfect Deflection — redirect the target's leverage to a third party of your choice | Diplomat ••• |

### Concession

Any character can concede at any point. The winner sets the broad outcome. The loser chooses the specific form. Conceding voluntarily before a track breaks carries no mechanical consequences beyond the concession itself.

A character who concedes with grace — who chooses how they back down — often comes out of a social conflict with more agency than one who fights to zero and has the outcome imposed on them.

**EXAMPLE — Social conflict — three-round exchange**

_Mira is confronting Mayor Fenn about the valley threat. Fenn's Social defense TN is 14. Social Scale 6 vs. Mira's effective Scale 5 (Status edge) — gap of 1, +2 to her effective TN: 16._

_Round 1, action 1: Mira Assesses. 'What does Fenn most fear losing from this conversation?' SG: his appearance of competent management. He needs to be seen handling this._

_Round 1, action 2: She Exposes Weakness — frames around his management of the situation, not the valley threat itself. Reason + Investigation vs. TN 10. Success. His defense TN drops to 13. Her effective TN is now 15._

_Round 1, action 3: She deploys Minor leverage (+1) from before the meeting and attacks. 3d6 + Poise (3) + Death Priest (3) + leverage (1) = 3d6+7 vs. TN 15. She rolls 8 — total 15. Clean success. Fenn takes 1 Composure damage._

_Round 2: Fenn uses Weight of Authority on a council aide — the aide visibly defers to Fenn's framing. SG spends 1 Taint: Mira gains Uncertain Read (−1 to next attack)._

_Round 2, Mira: attacks again (−2 repeat, −1 condition): 3d6+4 vs. TN 13. She rolls 9 — total 13. Clean success. Another Composure damage. Fenn is at Tier 1 now: −1 to all rolls._

_Round 3: Fenn concedes. He agrees publicly to commission an investigation — he does not promise to act on it. Mira set the terms. Fenn chose the form. The council heard everything._

## MENTAL CONFLICT

_\[ART: Elira across a desk from a nervous official — her journal open, documents between them, his discomfort visible\]_

Mental conflict targets the Willpower track. It is the domain of argument, investigation, psychological pressure, and any situation where someone's certainty is what's being broken down.

Mental conflict rewards preparation more than the other domains. Knowing what a target believes, what they fear, and what they're wrong about before pressing them is worth more than any raw roll bonus.

### Mental Actions

#### **Attack (Mental)**

Roll Reason or Guile + highest applicable skill vs. the target's Mental defense TN. On success, deal 1 Willpower damage + tier points spent.

#### **Analyze**

Identify a specific weakness in the target's reasoning or position. Unlike other actions, Analyze succeeds automatically — no roll required. The target's Mental defense TN is reduced by 1 until end of scene. You must articulate the weakness specifically — state what the gap in their position is. The SG confirms whether it's valid. Does not stack with itself unless each use identifies a genuinely different weakness.

#### **Reframe**

Shift the terms of the argument. Roll Guile or Reason + relevant skill vs. TN 10. On success, you choose which aspect of the issue is now under discussion. The target's preparation bonuses that don't apply to your framing are suspended until they spend an action re-establishing their position.

#### **Cite Precedent**

Invoke an authoritative source your target cannot dismiss without cost. Roll Reason + Scholar or equivalent vs. TN 8. On success, generate Minor leverage.

### Critical Effects in Mental Conflict

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Critical effect** | **Aspect requirement** |
| Methodical Collapse — use Analyze as a free action, ignoring up to 3 points of target's Mental defense TN for next attack | Scholar •• or Warden ••• |
| Cognitive Bleed — +1 Willpower damage AND target's Mental defense TN reduced by 1 for rest of scene | Any Mental Edge |
| Established Authority — target's Mental defense TN permanently reduced by 1 against you for this arc | Scholar ••• or Iron Reputation Edge |

**EXAMPLE — Mental conflict — preparation rewarded**

_Elira is pressing a forger about a disputed lineage document. Forger's Mental defense TN: 12._

_She prepared extensively: Quick Study (Scholar •) reduced prep time. She knows the cipher system and has identified three anachronisms in the document._

_Round 1, action 1: Assess. 'What is this person most afraid of having exposed?' SG: his own name appears in the archive records, implicating him in the original fraud._

_Round 1, action 2: Analyze. 'The watermark on this page didn't exist until six years after this document was supposedly written.' Succeeds automatically. TN drops to 11._

_Round 1, action 3: Attack. 3d6 + Reason (3) + Scholar (3) = 3d6+6 vs. TN 11. She rolls 7 — total 13. One tier above. 1 tier point spent on damage: 2 Willpower damage. Forger is Shaken (Tier 1). He admits the document was altered._

_Round 2: She activates Exploit Weakness (Scholar ••) on an Assess — tags him. Then attacks (−2 repeat): 3d6+4 vs. TN 11. She rolls 9 — total 13. Clean success + tagged +1 tier point. 3 Willpower damage._

_The forger Breaks. He tells her everything — including the name she wasn't looking for. She writes it down._

## SCALE

_\[ART: Side-by-side comparison — a farmer facing a knight on the left, a petitioner facing a duke on the right — TN gap annotated\]_

Scale measures the fundamental mismatch between entities operating at different magnitudes of power. A farmer petitioning a king, a student debating an ancient archive, a novice fighter facing a battle-hardened champion — these are conflicts where raw skill alone cannot bridge the gap. Scale applies across all three conflict domains.

This is not primarily about physical size. A freshly anointed knight and a skilled mercenary might have identical dice modifiers for a given contest, but they are not operating at the same level. The knight belongs to a different tier of physical actor. Scale makes that visible in the mechanics.

### Scale Ratings

Every character has three Scale ratings — one for each conflict domain.

• **Physical Scale** — your magnitude as a physical actor in conflict.

• **Social Scale** — your position in the hierarchies of authority and reputation.

• **Mental Scale** — the depth and reach of your knowledge and cognition.

Scale is recorded on your character sheet. Starting player characters have Scale 3 in all three domains — capable, established people who are already someone in the world. The scales run from 1 to 10.

Scale ratings are domain-specific. The same person will often have very different ratings across domains. A court scholar might have Physical Scale 2, Social Scale 5, and Mental Scale 7. A champion gladiator might have Physical Scale 7, Social Scale 2, and Mental Scale 3. Neither is better — they operate at different magnitudes in different spaces.

### The Scale Mechanic

When two parties are in conflict, compare their relevant Scale ratings. Each step of difference raises the lower party's Target Number by 2.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Scale gap** | **TN increase for lower party** |
| 0 (equal) | None |
| 1 step | +2  |
| 2 steps | +4  |
| 3 steps | +6  |
| 4 steps | +8  |
| 5+ steps | +10 or more |

The math does the rest. A farmer (Social Scale 2) petitioning a duke (Social Scale 7) faces a TN increase of +10 before anything else is considered. At that point, success without preparation, leverage, or a change of terms is not a failure of nerve — it is an accurate description of how power works.

There is no separate immunity rule. When the Scale gap is large enough, the TN simply exceeds what is reachable with the dice and modifiers available. The immunity is emergent, not declared. A TN of 22 is not 'you cannot act here' — it is 'you need more than you currently have.' That distinction keeps the door open for clever preparation without requiring a special exception in the rules.

### Scale Is Bidirectional — Automatically

Because the player makes all rolls — both attacks and defenses — the Scale gap applies in both directions without any additional calculation. A higher-Scale opponent is harder to damage and harder to avoid being damaged by. The same table handles both.

A capable fighter (Physical Scale 3) facing a champion (Physical Scale 6) has a gap of 3: +6 to TN in both directions. When the fighter attacks, the TN to deal damage is elevated by 6. When the champion acts against them, the fighter rolls their defense — and that TN is elevated by 6 as well. The champion's Scale advantage costs the player in both moments, from a single rule, with no additional work for the SG.

The SG never needs to track what an NPC 'rolls to attack.' The TN is set. Scale adjusts it. The player rolls.

### Circumstantial Modifiers

Scale ratings are permanent character facts, but effective Scale shifts with context. The hierarchy that granted your Scale has to be operating in the room for your Scale to apply at full value.

A knight arriving at the thieves' guild has Physical Scale that means little there and Social Scale that may actively work against him — the guild's hierarchy doesn't recognize his title or his backing. His effective Social Scale drops for the duration of that context. The underlying stat hasn't changed. His standing in this room has.

The SG calls circumstantial Scale modifiers before the roll, the same way TN modifiers are called. Circumstantial modifiers typically range from −2 to +2.

_Examples: A merchant captain is Social Scale 5 in any port. At sea, commanding her own ship and crew, she is effectively Social Scale 6 or 7. A scholar is Mental Scale 5 generally. In a library she built and has maintained for twenty years, she is effectively Mental Scale 7. A warrior is Physical Scale 5 in most conflicts. Chained, injured, and facing an executioner on familiar ground, she is effectively Physical Scale 3._

### Cross-Domain Influence

Scale advantage in one domain can generate a modifier in a conflict happening in a different domain, when the fiction clearly supports it.

The general rule: a party with a meaningful Scale advantage in one domain may, as an action, invoke that advantage to create a +1 or +2 modifier in a conflict currently happening in a different domain. The fiction must make this plausible. The SG confirms before the modifier is applied.

Cross-domain influence never replaces the primary domain of the conflict. It adds to it. And it always requires the fiction to support it.

**EXAMPLE — Cross-domain influence**

_A farmer is trying to persuade a knight to defend the village. The negotiation is going poorly — the knight sees no obligation here._

_Then Royce steps out of the barn. The knight can see immediately that he at least matches him in Physical Scale. No words spoken. The farmer's player asks whether Royce's presence does anything._

_The SG rules yes: Royce invokes his Physical Scale (5, equal to the knight) as cross-domain influence. +2 on the farmer's next social attack. The knight now has a concrete reason to reconsider that he didn't have thirty seconds ago._

### Piercing Scale

When the TN gap from Scale is large enough that success appears impossible, the lower party must change something before they can act effectively. They need to pierce Scale — not by rolling bigger numbers, but by changing what they bring to the conflict.

What piercing looks like depends on the domain:

**Physical:** A weapon specifically suited to a vulnerability. An ambush that negates the opponent's Physical advantages. Siege equipment against a fortified position.

**Social:** Leverage that operates at the Scale of the target. A formal introduction from someone the target must acknowledge. Institutional backing the target's hierarchy recognizes.

**Mental:** Preparation so thorough it closes the knowledge gap. A revelation that restructures the entire argument. Access to a primary source — a rare text, a living witness, a physical artifact — that grants standing the target cannot dismiss.

Piercing Scale doesn't erase the gap. It creates an opening. The TN is still elevated by the Scale difference. But a TN that was unreachable becomes difficult, and difficult is workable.

_The SG determines when the fiction supports piercing. The question is always: have you done something that earns you a shot?_

### The Three Scale Ladders

Each domain has its own named ladder. The names do the teaching — a player reading a stat block that says Fearsome (7) knows immediately what they're dealing with, even before the TN hits the table.

#### Physical Scale

Physical Scale measures your magnitude as a physical actor in conflict. Size, strength, speed, training, ferocity, and physical presence all contribute. Where these factors point in different directions, take the net picture.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale** | **Name** | **Examples** |
| 1   | Slight | A child, a bedridden elder, someone with no meaningful physical agency in conflict. |
| 2   | Common | Farmer, laborer, untrained adult. Present in a fight but unprepared for one. |
| 3   | Capable | Starting PC, fresh recruit, trained adult. Someone who belongs in the fight. |
| 4   | Seasoned | Veteran soldier, experienced fighter. A threatening animal — large wolf, bear. |
| 5   | Formidable | Elite soldier, peak athlete. A creature whose size or ferocity commands respect. Ogre territory. |
| 6   | Dangerous | Champion, apex predator. You do not engage this without a plan. |
| 7   | Fearsome | Legendary warrior, a giant, ancient apex predator. The kind of figure stories are told about. |
| 8   | Overwhelming | Something that changes the shape of a battlefield by existing on it. Elder creatures. War engines. |
| 9   | Mythic | Creatures that belong to another order of physical reality. Ancient dragons. Things that are events. |
| 10  | Titanic | World-scale forces. Entities that are, themselves, disasters. |

#### Social Scale

Social Scale measures your position in the hierarchies of authority, reputation, and institutional weight. It reflects not who you are personally, but where you stand in the structures that govern power in a given context.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale** | **Name** | **Examples** |
| 1   | Outcast | Exiles, pariahs, the deliberately erased. No standing recognized anywhere. |
| 2   | Invisible | Beggars, refugees, those the powerful do not see or bother to dismiss. |
| 3   | Common | Farmers, laborers, ordinary citizens. Present in the world, unremarkable within its power structures. PC baseline. |
| 4   | Established | Skilled tradespeople, minor merchants, respected locals. Someone whose name is known. |
| 5   | Notable | Guild officers, minor nobility, town officials. Someone who holds a position and is known to hold it. |
| 6   | Prominent | Major merchants, landed lords, high clergy. Someone the powerful notice and must acknowledge. |
| 7   | Powerful | Dukes, archbishops, heads of great houses. Commands institutional weight. |
| 8   | Commanding | Generals, royal advisors, heads of major institutions. Shapes events at scale. |
| 9   | Sovereign | Kings, emperors, supreme religious authorities. The hierarchy ends here. |
| 10  | Absolute | Living gods, divine mandates, world powers. Beyond appeal or meaningful opposition. |

#### Mental Scale

Mental Scale measures the depth and reach of your knowledge, reasoning, and cognitive engagement with a subject. It covers what you know, how well you can deploy it, and the institutional or epistemological weight behind it.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale** | **Name** | **Examples** |
| 1   | Uninformed | No relevant knowledge. Operating entirely in the dark on this subject. |
| 2   | Common | Basic education, folk wisdom, trade knowledge. What most people carry. |
| 3   | Studied | Formal training, apprenticeship, a well-read practitioner. PC baseline. |
| 4   | Expert | University scholars, master practitioners, seasoned professionals. Has earned the credential. |
| 5   | Authority | Renowned specialists, published theorists, leading voices in a field. Others cite them. |
| 6   | Institutional | Libraries, universities, intelligence networks with deep records. Knowledge organized at scale. |
| 7   | Archival | National archives, ancient orders, centuries of accumulated record and interpretation. |
| 8   | Encyclopedic | Vast interconnected systems of knowledge. Civilizational memory. |
| 9   | Oracular | Near-divine clarity. Ancient seers. Entities that know things they have no natural means of knowing. |
| 10  | Omniscient | Divine knowledge. Entities that simply know. |

_Assigning Scale to NPCs and creatures is a net judgment call. You weigh the relevant factors and land on a number. An ogre is large and powerful (pushes Physical Scale up) but slow and clumsy (pulls it back). A typical ogre lands at Physical Scale 4 or 5 — formidable enough to be genuinely threatening, not so high that capable characters have no options._

_When Scale is not listed for an NPC, default to 3 in all domains and adjust from there based on the fiction. A minor thug is Physical Scale 3 or 4, Social Scale 2, Mental Scale 2. A town magistrate is Physical Scale 3, Social Scale 5, Mental Scale 4. Trust your read of the character._

## EXTENDED CHALLENGES

_\[ART: A mountain pass in deteriorating weather — the party small against the landscape, one figure leading through the snow\]_

Not every conflict has a person on the other side. A blizzard crossing, a deteriorating manuscript, a flooding mine shaft, an angry crowd — these are conflicts where the opposition is a circumstance, a situation, or a collective. They have stakes. They have consequences. They resist.

The engine handles them with the same tools it uses for everything else. No separate subsystem. A TN, a track, and a break consequence. That's the whole thing.

### The Rule

To frame an extended challenge, establish three things before the first roll:

**The TN.** How difficult is each attempt? Set this the same way you set any TN — based on the fiction, the Scale of the opposition, and the resources available.

**The Track.** How much sustained effort can this challenge absorb before something breaks? Short tracks create fast, high-pressure challenges. Long tracks represent grinding endurance contests. Each successful roll fills boxes; failures may hold progress in place or — at SG discretion — drain them.

**The Break Consequence.** What happens when the track fills? This is the story beat the challenge is building toward. State it before play begins. The players should know what they're working toward and, where appropriate, what they're working against.

Players roll using whichever domain and skills apply to their approach. Different characters may contribute in different ways — one character forcing the pass physically while another navigates and a third manages the group's morale. All of it fills the same track.

_The break consequence is a story beat, not a termination. The track filling is the moment something changes — not necessarily something bad. A challenge can break toward the players as readily as against them. Define both possibilities when they exist._

### Calibrating the Track

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Track length** | **What it produces** |
| 3–4 boxes | A sharp, high-stakes moment. One or two failed rolls and the break is imminent. |
| 5–6 boxes | A sustained scene. Room for reversal and momentum shifts. |
| 7–10 boxes | A grind. Time pressure, resource attrition, morale as a factor. |
| 10+ boxes | An arc-length challenge. Reserved for campaign-defining obstacles. |

As a default, failed rolls hold the track in place — the characters aren't making progress, but they aren't losing ground. Reserve track drainage for challenges where failure actively makes things worse: a flooding room, a crowd growing more hostile, a patient whose condition is deteriorating.

### Scale in Extended Challenges

Scale applies to extended challenges the same way it applies to any conflict. The challenge has an effective Scale — the magnitude of what you're working against — and the gap between that Scale and the party's relevant Scale adjusts the TN.

A mountain blizzard is a Physical challenge. Its Scale reflects the severity of conditions: a hard snowstorm might be Physical Scale 4, a once-in-a-generation whiteout Physical Scale 7. A ranger with Physical Scale 5 faces a manageable gap against the first and a serious one against the second.

The same challenge reads differently for different parties. That's not a bug. Scale is doing its job.

#### Group Effort and Emergent Scale

When a group works together on an extended challenge, their coordination can generate emergent Scale — a temporary Scale bonus that reflects what organized collective action produces that individuals cannot.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Organized group size** | **Emergent Scale bonus** |
| 10–19 people | +1 (coordinated action, basic shared purpose) |
| 20–49 people | +2 (drilled or disciplined group) |
| 50–199 people | +3 (professional or highly motivated) |
| 200+ people | +4 (institutional scale) |

Emergent Scale requires two conditions: sufficient numbers and meaningful coordination. A panicked crowd of two hundred has no emergent Scale — they are two hundred individuals. A drilled work gang of thirty operating under a competent leader generates emergent Scale because they function as a single entity. If the leader falls or coordination breaks, emergent Scale drops immediately.

### Extended Challenges in Play

The following examples show the system working across different domains.

**EXAMPLE — The Mountain Crossing**

_The party needs to cross Ashridge Pass before the storm closes it. Physical Scale 5, TN 14, track 6 boxes. Break consequence: track fills — through and exhausted. Track drains to zero — driven back or stranded._

_Sera leads navigation: 3d6 + Reflex (2) + Skald (3) = 3d6+5 vs. TN 14. Success fills a box. She also spots a shepherd's shelter two hundred yards off the path._

_Elira contributes her knowledge of mountain weather patterns. Her Vocation applies marginally. She rolls — success at cost: good guidance but she takes Tier 1 physical damage from the exposure._

_Royce rolls to keep the group moving through the worst of it. Success. Another box. He says nothing. He just moves._

_Five successful rolls later, the track is full. They're through. The shelter Sera spotted is where they spend the night. The world remembers they crossed this in a storm._

**EXAMPLE — The Crumbling Record**

_A merchant's ledger holds evidence of a conspiracy, but the ink is fading. One scene before it's unreadable. Mental challenge, effective Scale 5, TN 12, track 5 boxes. Break consequence: track fills — enough recovered to use as evidence. Track drains to zero — document is gone._

_Elira leads. Scholar Vocation applies directly. 3d6 + Reason (3) + Scholar (3) = 3d6+6 vs. TN 12. Clean success. Two boxes filled — she identifies the cipher key._

_Mira recognizes the merchant's guild marks from her Fellowship's contact network. Her Interest in Spiritual Lore is a stretch, but the SG allows it for the guild heraldry specifically. Another box._

_A failed roll. The ink smears in handling. The SG rules drain triggers: one box lost. The track resets to two filled._

_Elira rolls again with the cipher key. Success plus: she recovers the names and figures, and notices a second signature she wasn't looking for. The track fills. The document is gone. What they have is enough._

**EXAMPLE — The Mob**

_A crowd has gathered outside the magistrate's hall. Two hundred people with a shared grievance and nothing to lose — Social Scale 5 from emergent Scale. TN 12, track 6 boxes. Two break directions: track fills through persuasion — crowd parts and a spokesperson emerges who has the magistrate's ear. Track drains to zero — mood breaks wrong, situation becomes physical._

_Mira steps forward and acknowledges the crowd's grievance before asking anything of them. Social attack: 3d6 + Influence (2) + Death Priest (3) vs. TN 12. Success: two boxes. The nearest people stop shouting._

_Royce moves to the edge of the crowd — not threatening, just visible. The player asks if his Physical Scale (5) does anything. The SG rules yes: cross-domain influence, +1 on Mira's next roll. His presence signals someone here can handle trouble without making it._

_Mira deploys what she knows: the merchant has a specific contract with the guild she can name. Minor leverage. +1. She frames this as information the crowd can use. Success plus: three boxes filled. A baker near the front starts repeating what Mira said to the people behind her._

_Elira tries to explain the legal mechanism for price-fixing complaints. The SG sets drain on failure: if she fails, the mood slips. She fails by two tiers. A box drains. Someone shouts something unflattering about people who talk like lawyers. Elira writes this down._

_Mira pivots immediately — asks the baker what she needs the magistrate to actually do. One question, the right question. Success plus: the last boxes fill. The baker turns to the crowd. They part._

_The magistrate, watching from a window, has seen the whole thing. That matters too._

### Break Consequences

The break consequence is the story beat the challenge was building toward. Write it before the first roll — not as a punishment waiting at the end, but as a commitment about what this conflict means in the fiction.

Break consequences should be specific. Not 'the party fails' but 'the ship makes port but the hull is breached and will not sail again without significant repair.' Not 'the crowd turns hostile' but 'the magistrate locks the doors and the players are now associated with the riot in the morning's account of events.'

When the challenge can break in two directions — toward the players or against them — define both before play begins. The players' choices during the challenge influence which way it falls. Give them enough information to play toward an outcome, not just to roll dice and wait.

A break consequence that resolves into a new situation — a new ally, a new enemy, a door that's now open or closed — is better than one that simply ends the scene. Extended challenges work best when they generate the next problem rather than concluding the current one.

## NPC CONSTRUCTION

NPCs are built for the SG's ease, not mechanical completeness. Most NPCs exist for one scene. The stat block format scales with how much the NPC matters — from a group of guards handled as one entity to a world-defining figure who recurs across an entire campaign.

Four tiers: Mook, Elite, Boss, and Legend. Only Legends use full PC-equivalent construction. Everything below that uses streamlined formats.

### Mooks

Mooks are undifferentiated opposition — guards, bandits, cultists, wildlife in numbers. Handled as groups rather than individuals. The Mook Group rules are in Part Four-A. This section covers construction.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stat** | **Typical range** | **Notes** |
| TN  | 8–12 | Covers all actions. Drops by 1 per member lost. |
| Threshold | 2–3 | Damage needed to drop one member. Higher = more resilient individually. |
| Size | 3–6 | Starting group size. |
| Scale | Physical 2–4 | List all three domain Scale ratings. Most Mooks are Physical Scale 3–4, Social Scale 2, Mental Scale 2. |
| Trait | Optional | One word or one sentence. Only include if it changes how the group fights. |

**EXAMPLE — Mook Group stat blocks**

_CITY WATCH — TN: 10 | Threshold: 2 | Size: 4 | Scale: Physical 3, Social 3, Mental 2_

_Trait: Backup Coming — if the fight lasts more than 3 rounds, a second group of equal size arrives._

_PALE VANGUARD SOLDIERS — TN: 12 | Threshold: 2 | Size: 6 | Scale: Physical 4, Social 3, Mental 2_

_Trait: Disciplined — do not rout until Size drops to 1. Bone talismans: the first soldier killed rises again unless the talisman is destroyed._

### Elites

Elites are individually notable opponents — a skilled duelist, a veteran mercenary captain, a gifted orator. They have individual stat blocks but no full character sheet.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Stat** | **Notes** |
| TN  | Single TN covering all actions in their primary domain. Secondary domain TN is 2 lower. |
| Scale | List all three. Elites typically have Scale 4–5 in their primary domain. |
| Health track | One relevant track. Hits equal to track size = Elite is defeated. No tier penalties — Elites fight at full capacity until they drop. |
| Trait | 1–2 traits. Each does one specific thing. Keep them simple. |
| Goal | One sentence. What does this NPC want from this scene? |

**EXAMPLE — Elite stat block**

_HARVEL DOSS, REGIONAL MERCHANT_

_TN: 14 (Social) / 12 (Mental) / 10 (Physical)_

_Scale: Physical 3, Social 5, Mental 4_

_Composure track: 5 boxes_

_Trait: Patient — Harvel never deploys leverage immediately. He waits one round before using any leverage he holds. If the players don't press him, he uses it at maximum effect._

_Trait: Good Accounts — once per scene, Harvel may produce specific financial information that functions as Major leverage on any party member who owes debts in the region._

_Goal: Establish that the Dragon Flagon property is commercially viable for redevelopment and that the party's tenure is temporary._

### Bosses

Bosses are significant recurring antagonists or major scene opponents. They have full stat blocks across all three domains and persist across multiple scenes or sessions.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Stat** | **Notes** |
| TN  | Separate TN for each domain. Set based on their actual capability in each. |
| Scale | All three domains. Bosses typically have Scale 5–7 in their primary domain. |
| Health tracks | All three tracks with full tier penalties. Bosses break like player characters. |
| Traits | 2–4 traits. At least one should create a tactical problem the party needs to solve. |
| Edges | 2–3 relevant Edges from the catalog. Use full Edge text. |
| Goal | One paragraph. What do they want? What would make them concede? What won't they concede under any circumstances? |

**EXAMPLE — Boss stat block**

_MAYOR ALDOUS FENN_

_TN: 8 (Physical) / 12 (Mental) / 16 (Social — in Goldenfields context)_

_Scale: Physical 3, Social 6, Mental 4_

_Health tracks: Vitality 4 | Willpower 5 | Composure 7_

_Trait: Institutional Weight — as long as Fenn holds his position as Mayor, all Social attacks against him in Goldenfields suffer +2 to TN. This does not apply outside the town's formal structures._

_Trait: Never Says Anything Actionable — Fenn's attacks never generate evidence. He never does anything that can be used against him directly. Leverage against him must be generated through investigation of his associates, not his own actions._

_Trait: Harvel in the Back — when Harvel Doss is present in the same scene, Fenn's Social Scale is treated as 7 instead of 6. Their cooperation is worth more than either individually._

_Goal: Remain the unchallenged voice of Goldenfields. Not because he loves the town — because his identity depends on being its manager. He will concede tactical ground but not structural authority._

### Legends

Legends are the setting's defining figures — the powers that shaped the world before the players arrived and will shape it long after. Building a Legend is not a mechanical exercise. It is a world-building exercise that happens to have mechanical expression.

Build Legends between sessions. A Legend should be a character you know well before the players encounter them.

Legends use full PC-equivalent character creation: Attributes, Skills, Edges, Aspects, Conviction, Path — all built using the same rules as player characters. Setting supplements define Legend-exclusive Aspects that go beyond the core engine's trees.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Legend design question** | **What to decide** |
| What did this Legend do? | Not 'they were powerful' but 'this specific thing happened because of them.' |
| What do they want now? | How has that changed from when they were at the height of their power? |
| What do they fear? | What would it cost them to lose, and what would they do to prevent that loss? |
| How do they see the party? | Pawns? Threats? Potential allies? Irritants? |
| What is the encounter about? | Not 'defeat the Boss' but 'what story does this encounter tell?' |

Legends frequently operate at Scale 3 to 5 in their primary domain. This places them qualitatively outside normal engagement. A character trying to fight a Scale 5 Legend directly will find the TN unreachable without a specific vulnerability or approach.

This is the point. Engaging a Legend narratively before engaging them mechanically is almost always correct. Find the vulnerability. Change the terms. Bring something that makes the Scale gap irrelevant. The Legend's Scale is not a barrier to overcome by rolling big numbers — it is a signal that the story needs a different approach.

The mechanical core of a Legend is less important than knowing who this person is. A Legend with a detailed personality and clear wants is interesting in every scene. A Legend with optimal mechanics but no defined character is only interesting in combat.

## CORRUPTION AND TAINT

Corruption is a character-side degradation track. It does not modify your dice or impose penalties on your rolls. Instead, it feeds the SG's Taint pool — the outward pressure that Corruption generates into the world. The more Corruption a character carries, the more the world pushes back.

Thematically, Corruption is the toll that power, darkness, and moral compromise take on a person. A character who uses powerful Aspects, handles dangerous artifacts, and makes choices their Conviction forbids will accumulate Corruption. That Corruption doesn't weaken them in the moment — it makes the world more hostile over time.

_Different settings use different names for Corruption. In Wrath & Woe it is called Despair. In Drakorath it is Compulsion. In Ghost Code it is Dissonance. The mechanics are the same. The flavoring belongs to the world._

### Corruption Sources

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source** | **Amount** | **When** |
| First Corruption | 1   | When a complication triggers and the character currently has zero Corruption. |
| Break | 1   | When any health track reaches zero. |
| Overflow damage | 1 (capped 1/round) | When a maxed track takes additional damage. |
| High-dot Aspect activation | 1 (dots 3–4) or 2 (dot 5) | Automatic on use. Not a roll. |
| Environmental exposure | 1   | Potency 1–5 source. Resisted once per scene with Endurance roll vs. potency TN. |
| Conviction-linked trigger | 1   | When the character acts against their core Conviction. Collaborative — not punitive. |
| Path Collapse | 1+  | When a character betrays their Path under specific conditions. See Part Two. |

### The Taint Pool

Every Corruption gain feeds the SG's Taint pool immediately. The SG gains 1 Taint whenever any player character gains Corruption. The Taint pool starts each session at a baseline determined by the mission's risk rating plus the number of players.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Taint spend** | **Cost** | **Effect** |
| Force Luck swap | 1   | Force a player to swap one Result Die with the Luck Die. The SG chooses which Result Die. |
| Raise NPC TN | 1–2 | Raise one NPC's TN by 1 or 2 for the current scene. State a fictional reason first. |
| Add complication | 1   | Introduce something that goes wrong alongside a success. Separate from the roll outcome. |
| Tier point on NPC action | 1   | Add 1 tier point to an NPC action when a player fails their defense roll (cap 3 total). |
| Bank toward Tides | Any | Unspent Taint moves to the arc bank for Tide advancement. |

The Taint pool is the SG's primary active tool during play. It should be spent freely — a SG who hoards Taint isn't using the system as intended. Taint that isn't spent or banked at session end is lost. Spend it or lose it.

### Shattered Hearth

Shattered Hearth is the engine's permanent consequence mechanism. It fires when three conditions are simultaneously true: a character's health track is at zero (they are Broken), a complication triggers on a roll (Result Dice total 2 or 3), and the character has at least 1 Corruption.

When Shattered Hearth fires, the player chooses a permanent consequence from the menu below. This choice is theirs — not the SG's. The SG sets the severity tier based on the circumstances. The player decides which form the consequence takes within that tier.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Severity tier** | **When** | **Consequence options (player chooses one)** |
| Minor Scar | First Shattered Hearth, low Corruption | A lasting behavioral tic. A phobia (Burden •). A relationship permanently damaged. A capability slightly diminished. |
| Significant Scar | Repeated Shattered Hearth or moderate Corruption | A Burden at ••. A lost connection (contact gone). A skill tier permanently inaccessible in one specific context. A permanent −1 to one attribute. |
| Major Scar | Shattered Hearth at high Corruption | A Burden at ••• or higher. Loss of an Edge permanently. Path Collapse (see Part Two). A significant external loss — property, allies, standing. |

Shattered Hearth is not death. It is not the end of the character. It is the mark left by the moment when everything went wrong at once. The character continues. The scar is permanent.

_Path Collapse is a consequence option, not an automatic outcome. When Shattered Hearth fires, the player chooses from the severity-tiered menu. Path Collapse is one of the options at the Significant and Major tiers — choose it because it feels right for the character and the moment, not because it's the only option available._

### Reducing Corruption

Corruption reduces through Path-linked activities — the specific conditions defined by each character's Path that represent recovery and renewal. In general, Corruption reduces when a character actively honors their core orientation: the Steward keeps something alive that needed keeping, the Wanderer finds something by going toward it, the Defiant makes refusal cost the person who expected compliance.

Corruption does not reduce passively with time. It requires active engagement with what makes the character who they are.

Reducing Corruption takes one step per qualifying activity. There is no mechanic that clears Corruption in bulk — a character who has accumulated significant Corruption will need to do significant work to shed it.

## BREAK AND ITS CONSEQUENCES

When a health track reaches zero, the character has Broken in that domain. Breaking is not death. It is a threshold — a significant story moment that creates consequences, permanent or temporary, based on how it's handled.

### Voluntary vs Involuntary Break

Any character can choose to Break voluntarily before their track actually hits zero. This is concession expressed through the body rather than words — the fighter who drops their weapon and falls to one knee, the negotiator who finally cracks and admits what they know, the scholar who can no longer maintain their position under the weight of evidence.

Voluntary Break carries no Shattered Hearth risk — the complication condition cannot fire on a voluntary act. It also ends the conflict for that character in that domain, which may or may not be advantageous.

Involuntary Break — the track hitting zero through accumulated damage — triggers the Break state and may trigger Shattered Hearth if the other conditions are met.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Break state** | **Domain** | **What it means in play** |
| Felled | Physical | −3 to all rolls. 1 action per round. Cannot fight effectively. Not dead. Can still act within limits. |
| Broken | Mental | −3 to all rolls. 1 action per round. Cannot maintain convictions. May be compelled to concede positions they would otherwise hold. |
| Fallen | Social | −3 to all rolls. 1 action per round. Social standing effectively destroyed in the relevant context. |

### Recovery from Break

**Tier 1 damage** clears with a few minutes of rest at the end of a scene. No roll required.

**Tier 2 damage** requires a full scene of downtime and attention — bandaging, sleep, or appropriate care.

**Break (Tier 3 / track at zero)** requires significant recovery time and, in some cases, specific narrative resolution. A character who is Felled physically may recover with rest and medical care. A character who is Broken mentally may not recover until they address what broke them. A character who is Fallen socially needs to do something in the fiction that begins to rebuild their standing.

The SG should not simply clear a Break at the end of a scene. The fiction should acknowledge it. Recovery is not automatic — it is earned.

## TIDES

_\[ART: A map with several marked locations, some circled, threads connecting them — a SG's planning document visible from above\]_

Tides are active threats in the world that move independently of the players. They are not plots waiting to be triggered. They are situations already in motion, progressing at their own pace, indifferent to whether the players are paying attention.

Tides advance when the SG spends from the arc Taint bank at arc end. The spending is mechanical; the advancement is fictional. What changes in the world is always described in fiction — a faction takes a new position, a disease spreads to the next valley, a building that could have been saved isn't.

Tides make the world feel alive. They ensure that the players' decisions to focus here mean neglecting there. They create the pressure that makes choice meaningful.

_Tides are SG-side documents. Players may know a Tide exists — a visible threat is known. But the track state, the intermediate milestones, and the specific consequences are not shared by default. Some Tides should be entirely hidden until they advance into something the players can see._

### Tide Design

Start with the completion milestone — what happens if the players never intervene. Make it genuinely bad. Not 'mildly inconvenient' bad, but 'the world changes in a way the players will feel' bad. Then work backward through 3 to 4 intermediate milestones.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Tide design question** | **What to decide** |
| What is this threat? | A faction, a person, a situation, a force of nature. Give it a name and a goal. |
| What does it want? | The completion state. Write this first. Be specific. |
| How does it get there? | 3–4 intermediate milestones. Each should feel like real progress, not just flavor. |
| What can players find? | At each milestone, what clues are visible to attentive players? Make these findable without telegraphing. |
| What would stop it? | One or two specific interventions that would set the Tide back or end it. Players shouldn't need to know these, but you should. |

### The Arc Taint Bank

During each session, the SG may bank any amount of unspent Taint toward the arc bank instead of spending it in-session. At arc end, the SG spends the arc bank on Tide advancement: each Tide milestone costs a number of banked Taint equal to its difficulty (typically 1–3).

Taint remaining in the arc bank at arc end that isn't spent on Tide advancement is lost. The bank does not carry between arcs. This creates a rhythm: pressure builds during an arc, the world responds at arc end, the next arc begins with fresh pressure.

Tides that are actively engaged by the players may be slowed or halted without Taint spending — if the players directly address a Tide in play, the SG should reflect that in whether it advances.

### Running Multiple Tides

A typical campaign has 2 to 4 active Tides at once. More than that and the world starts to feel like it's constantly collapsing. Fewer than 2 and the world feels static.

Tides should have different timeframes. One Tide might complete in a single arc if the players don't engage it. Another might be a slow-burning multi-arc threat. The variation creates natural pacing rhythm — immediate urgent problems alongside longer structural threats.

Not every Tide needs player engagement to be interesting. Some Tides should advance whether or not the players notice, creating a world that changes without their direction. This is what makes discovery matter.

### Tide Signals and Discovery

Each Tide should have visible signs at each milestone that attentive players can find. These are not announcements — they are clues available to players who are looking. The signs should make sense in retrospect. When a Tide reaches its completion and players look back, they should be able to trace the warning signs they missed or ignored.

Never punish players for missing Tide signals. The world moves whether they watch it or not. That's not punishment — that's consequence. The distinction matters at the table.

**EXAMPLE — Tide design — The Deepvein Thread**

_Completion: Harvel Doss acquires the Dragon Flagon property through legitimate channels after the party defaults on a debt they didn't know existed. The Anchor is lost. Roswyn is out. The party loses their home base._

_Milestone 1 (Taint cost 1): Harvel's factor quietly purchases the outstanding debt from the previous owner's estate. The party doesn't know this exists. Elira's accounting pass found a hint — a name in old records. If she pursues it: she finds the debt._

_Milestone 2 (Taint cost 2): The debt is called in. A formal notice arrives for Roswyn. She tells the party immediately. They have one arc to respond. This milestone is announced — the players now know the Tide exists._

_Milestone 3 (Taint cost 2): Fenn uses the debt situation to call a town council vote on the Dragon Flagon's operating license. The Standing track takes 1 damage as the community's uncertainty solidifies._

_Completion (Taint cost 3): The council votes. Without party intervention — paying the debt, discrediting Harvel, or building enough Standing to override the vote — the property transfers._

_What would stop it: Paying the debt (costs 3 wealth points, which the party probably doesn't have). Discrediting Harvel through the Deepvein Trading Company connection. Building Standing to 3 boxes before the vote._

## THE ANCHOR

_\[ART: The Dragon Flagon interior — warm firelight, maps and mugs on the table, Roswyn visible behind the bar, the space clearly inhabited and cared for\]_

An Anchor is the party's home base — the place they return to, invest in, defend, and build into something that matters. It is not just a safe house or a convenient address. It is a stake in the world. A reason to care what happens to a specific place and the people in it.

What makes an Anchor distinct from any other location is investment — the party's time, wealth, and decisions have shaped it. It has a history that includes them. And it can be threatened, damaged, and lost in ways that matter to the story because they matter to the players.

Anchors are earned, not purchased. The first Anchor typically arrives as a reward, an inheritance, a gift with strings attached, or the consequence of something the party did. The world gives them a place because they did something worth rewarding. What they do with it is the next chapter.

_Design note: An Anchor that was bought with wealth points is a property. An Anchor that was earned through play is a home. The distinction is not mechanical — the rules work the same either way — but it is real. Tables that earn their first Anchor remember how they got it._

### The Three Tracks

Every Anchor has three tracks mirroring the engine's three conflict domains. Each track has boxes equal to the Anchor's tier rating in that domain. Tracks can be damaged, repaired, and upgraded. When a track breaks, consequences follow.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Track** | **Domain** | **What it represents** |
| Condition | Physical | The Anchor's structural and operational health. The roof, the walls, the equipment, the people who maintain it. A damaged Condition track means the place is visibly deteriorating — or under active physical threat. |
| Standing | Social | The Anchor's reputation and institutional weight in the local area. How the community sees it, what factions recognize it, whether it has goodwill or suspicion working for it. A damaged Standing track means the place is distrusted, associated with failure, or actively opposed by local power. |
| Stores | Resources | The Anchor's material resources — wealth points held in reserve, supplies, equipment, and consumable assets the party can draw on between sessions. A damaged Stores track means the party is drawing on empty reserves. |

### Track Damage and Break

Each track has three tiers of impairment, identical in structure to character health tracks. As boxes are lost, the Anchor's capabilities in that domain degrade.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tier** | **Condition** | **Standing** | **Stores** |
| Tier 1 (half empty) | Visible neglect. Minor penalties to actions taken from the Anchor. | Locals are uncertain. Circumstantial Social Scale bonus reduced by 1. | Resources are thin. Party draws at −1 wealth point per downtime action. |
| Tier 2 (1–2 boxes left) | Serious disrepair. Anchor-based actions suffer −1 to rolls. | Community trust is wavering. Standing Scale bonus lost. | Stores nearly exhausted. Downtime actions that require resources may not be possible. |
| Break (zero boxes) | The Anchor is non-functional as a base. Cannot be used for rest, recovery, or preparation. | The Anchor's social standing is destroyed. Active opposition in local area. | The Anchor is destitute. No resource generation until Stores are rebuilt. |

A broken track is a story event, not a termination. The Anchor still exists. The party still owns it. But something has to change before it functions again.

### Anchor Break Consequences

When an Anchor track breaks, the table chooses a consequence from a menu — or proposes something that fits the fiction better. The SG sets the severity. The players choose the form. This is the same mechanic as Shattered Hearth, applied to the party's shared home.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Track break** | **Consequence options (table chooses one)** |
| Condition Break | Major structural damage requiring 3+ wealth points to repair. Loss of a specific facility. A resident NPC is injured or leaves. The Anchor must be abandoned temporarily. |
| Standing Break | A faction publicly opposes the Anchor. A key community relationship is severed. The party is associated with the failure in the local area's memory. Access to community resources is revoked. |
| Stores Break | A debt is called in. A resource that was available is permanently gone. A supplier relationship ends. The party starts the next arc with 0 wealth points. |

### Anchor Scale

An Anchor has a Scale rating in each domain, reflecting its capacity to support the party's operations. A newly established Anchor starts at Scale 2 in all domains — below functional. Scale increases as the party invests.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anchor tier** | **Scale in each domain** | **What becomes available** |
| 0 (acquired) | 1–2 | Basic shelter. No mechanical benefits. The work starts here. |
| 1 (functional) | 2–3 | Basic rest and recovery. Downtime actions possible. 1 wealth point per arc generated. |
| 2 (established) | 3–4 | Full downtime menu available. Circumstantial Scale bonus in local area. 2 wealth points per arc. |
| 3 (prominent) | 4–5 | Active community support. Meaningful resource generation. Factions recognize it. |
| 4 (renowned) | 5–6 | Regional significance. Political weight. 4+ wealth points per arc. |
| 5 (legendary) | 6+  | The Anchor is itself a faction. Its reputation precedes the party. |

### Improving the Anchor

Anchors improve through investment — downtime actions and wealth point expenditure. Improving a track requires a combination of both.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Action** | **Cost** | **Effect** |
| Repair (Condition) | 1–2 downtime actions + 1 wealth point | Restore 1 Condition box. Multiple actions may be needed for significant damage. |
| Outreach (Standing) | 1–2 downtime actions | Restore 1 Standing box through relationship maintenance and community engagement. |
| Provision (Stores) | 1 wealth point | Restore 1 Stores box. |
| Develop (upgrade) | 3 downtime actions + 2 wealth points | Add one upgrade to the Anchor (see below). Counts toward tier advancement. |
| Tier advancement | Complete all upgrades for current tier | Anchor tier increases. Track sizes grow. Scale improves. |

Anchors can have specific upgrades that add mechanical capabilities: a Functioning Kitchen (provides Tier 1 healing during rest scenes), a Secure Vault (enables wealth point storage), an Intelligence Network (provides information-gathering between sessions). The SG and players define what upgrades make sense for their specific Anchor.

### Tides and the Anchor

The Anchor is a natural target for Tide pressure. When the SG spends Taint against a resource the party actively manages, the Anchor is often the right place for it to land. The Standing track is most vulnerable — faction opposition, council maneuvering, and whisper campaigns all attack it. The Condition track is vulnerable to physical threats. The Stores track is vulnerable to financial pressure.

The personal/world distinction applies to the Anchor the same way it applies to personal connections. Neglect and misuse are always signaled before they take effect. World events — a faction shifting its position, a merchant acquiring a relevant debt — are not announced. Players discover them when they interact with the Anchor and find things changed.

**EXAMPLE — Dragon Flagon — first downtime after Session One**

_After their first session, the party takes downtime. The Dragon Flagon's starting state: Condition 2/3 filled, Standing 1/3 filled, Stores 1/3 filled._

_Royce takes two Repair actions on Condition. He hires a carpenter for the first (1 wealth point) — the carpenter fixes the roof. He patches the cellar door and cleans the hearth flue himself for the second. Condition track restored to full._

_Mira takes one Outreach action on Standing. She sits with older residents who remember the tavern in its heyday, asks about the building's history, and does a vigil for a resident who recently died. One Standing box restored. She also learns: the Deepvein Trading Company has history with the previous owner._

_Elira takes one Manage action — she goes through Roswyn's accounts. She prevents the neglect tick that would have fired at arc end. She also finds: a debt owed to the Dragon Flagon from a merchant who assumed Roswyn would never collect._

_Sera takes one Provision action (1 wealth point to Stores, restoring 1 box) and begins a Develop action — first contribution toward a Functioning Kitchen upgrade._

_End of downtime: the Anchor is in better shape than they found it. The Deepvein thread is in Elira's journal. The kitchen project is started. Fenn is already asking questions at council._

## DOWNTIME

Downtime represents the activity characters pursue between sessions — the maintenance, preparation, and personal work that happens in the spaces between adventures. It is not rest. It is the work that makes the next session possible.

### The Downtime Bank

Characters receive 3 downtime actions per downtime period, regardless of its length. The SG determines how long the downtime period is in the fiction — a week between sessions, a month while the party recovers, a season while waiting for an event. The number of actions does not change. Expanded downtime rules are in the Storyteller Guide.

Downtime points that aren't spent during an arc's downtime period do not carry forward. Use them or lose them — the same principle that applies to Taint.

### Downtime Actions

The following actions are available to all characters. Setting supplements may add additional options specific to their world.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Action** | **Cost** | **What it does** |
| Repair (Anchor) | 1 downtime + optional wealth | Restore 1 Condition box on the party Anchor. Wealth point spend allows hiring help for faster or more significant repairs. |
| Outreach (Anchor) | 1–2 downtime | Restore 1 Standing box through relationship maintenance, community engagement, or active reputation work. |
| Provision (Anchor) | 1 wealth point | Restore 1 Stores box. |
| Develop (Anchor upgrade) | 1 downtime (of 3 needed) + 2 wealth points total | Contribute toward an Anchor upgrade. Full upgrade requires 3 downtime actions and 2 wealth points total. |
| Connection repair | 2 downtime points | Improve one connection health track by 1 step (from Distant to Warm, etc.). |
| Research (mental prep) | 1 downtime | Gain +1 to one specific roll type during the next session. Declare in advance what was researched. |
| Taint resolution | 1 downtime + SG approval | Address a specific Corruption source. If successful, Corruption reduces by 1. Requires fictional justification. |
| Advancement prep | 1 downtime | Establish prospective justification for one pending XP purchase. The SG approves or redirects. |
| Wealth generation | 1 downtime + Lifestyle Edge | Generate wealth points above your standard arc income through active trade, contracts, or work. |

_Downtime is deliberately modest. The Willowlight Engine rewards preparation but doesn't require it. A character who doesn't take downtime is not disadvantaged by the rules — they simply don't have the preparation bonuses and Anchor investment that a character who did prepare has. The world moves either way. Downtime lets you steer some of that motion._

## CONNECTIONS

Connections are the relationships that define a character's place in the world. They include starter contacts, Edge-purchased Contacts and Allies, and any relationships that develop through play. All connections use the same health track system.

### Connection Health

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Health** | **State** | **What it means** | **Maintenance required** |
| 3   | Strong | Active and reciprocal. The contact goes out of their way for you. | None |
| 2   | Warm | Solid. The relationship is in good standing. | Minor — occasional contact |
| 1   | Distant | The relationship has cooled. The contact is less forthcoming. | Active — 1 downtime point per arc |
| 0   | Strained | The contact is reluctant. −1 to rolls relying on them. | Significant — 2 downtime points to repair |
| —   | Lost | The connection is gone. | Cannot be restored through normal downtime |

### Connection Health Decay

Connection health ticks down under three conditions. The first two are always signaled to the player before they take effect. The third is not.

**Neglect** — No meaningful contact across an arc. Health ticks down 1 step at arc end. The SG signals this before it happens: 'You haven't reached out to Corvin in weeks. If this continues, the relationship will cool.'

**Misuse** — The character endangered, exploited, or disrespected the connection. Health ticks down 1–2 steps depending on severity. Immediate, not arc-end. Signaled.

**World events (Taint spend)** — The SG spends 1 Taint at session end. Off-screen events damage the relationship. Not signaled — players discover this when they next interact with the contact.

_Signal before loss: the personal/world distinction. Neglect and misuse are things the player can control — signal before they take effect. World events are part of the world moving independently. The player learns about them when they reach out next time and find things changed. This is not punishment. It is how the world works._

### Connection Health Improvement

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Method** | **Effect** |
| Downtime action — connection repair | 2 downtime points: improve 1 health step |
| Significant in-play moment | Saving the contact, fulfilling a major obligation, a genuine character beat: +1 step, SG awards |
| Gift or favor | A meaningful gesture delivered in fiction: +1 step, once per arc per contact |

### What Connections Provide

The strength of a connection determines what you can ask of it. A Warm (health 2) contact answers your questions and helps when it's reasonable. A Strong (health 3) contact goes out of their way, takes risks for you, and treats your problems as their problems. A Distant (health 1) contact helps but is guarded and may ask what they're getting out of it.

**Information** — Once per session, ask your contact a question in their area of knowledge. They answer honestly within what they know. Health 1+ required.

**Job hooks** — Contacts may offer work, favors, or leads between arcs. Health 2+ typically required for proactive outreach.

**Active assistance** — Contacts who are Strong (health 3) may join scenes, provide resources, or take risks on your behalf.

**Reciprocal obligation** — All contacts may call on the character for help in turn. These are obligations, not merely benefits.

## SECRETS

_Containment doesn’t make the world forget. It only delays when the world speaks._

A Secret is something being actively hidden. Not backstory — a live obligation. The world was present when the thing happened. People, animals, objects, and in some settings the land itself carry the impression of what occurred. No amount of resource expenditure erases that impression. Containment buys time. It does not buy silence.

A Secret is not defined by whether the thing hidden was bad. A hidden love, a secret heir, a mercy not sanctioned by law — these carry Weight and Spread the same as a massacre. What defines a Secret is that someone is working to prevent the world from knowing. The content shapes the consequences when it exposes. The mechanics are the same regardless.

The Spread track never moves backward. A character who successfully contains a secret for three arcs has not reduced what the world knows. They have delayed when that knowledge surfaces. Play accordingly.

### Recording a Secret

Secrets are recorded when the players and SG agree something is significant enough to track, narratively relevant, and will deepen the story. This is a collaborative judgment, not a mandatory rule. Not every bad act becomes a Secret. Not every hidden thing needs a track.

Secrets can be recorded at any point — immediately after the event, or several sessions later when the table recognizes that a thread has weight worth formalizing. They can also be retired: if a storyline ends, a thread dies, or the table agrees the secret has gone stale, it can be dropped without firing consequences. The SG and players manage this together.

When a Secret is recorded, the player writes it on their sheet and the SG notes it separately. Two values are set: **Weight** and **starting Spread**. The SG sets both and tells the player. Starting Spread is 1 by default but may be set higher if the fiction demands it — if the event was witnessed, if word has already moved, if the character arrives at the table with something that was never fully contained.

### Weight

Weight measures the severity of consequences if the Secret fully exposes. It reflects who cares, what they can do about it, and how much the world changes when the truth comes out. Weight is set by the SG and does not change unless the fiction genuinely changes what exposure means.

Weight is not a measure of moral severity. A defied lord’s writ may outweigh a burned village if the lord treats defiance as the graver offense. The question is never what was done. It is what the people who find out will do.

| **Weight** | **What it means** | **Taint cost to advance Spread** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1   | Consequences are personal or local. A few people would act on this if they knew. Life becomes harder but not unrecoverable. | 1 Taint per step |
| 2   | Consequences reach factions, public standing, or relationships that matter at scale. People with real power want to know. | 2 Taint per step |
| 3   | Exposure changes everything. The forces that want this information are serious, motivated, and patient. Containing it is a constant drain. | 3 Taint per step |

### Spread

Spread tracks how widely the secret has moved. The character is fighting to keep it from advancing. They are not fighting to reduce it.

| **Spread** | **State** | **What it means in play** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1   | Whispered | The secret is held. Almost no one outside the character’s immediate circle has any piece of it. |
| 2   | Known | Something is out. The world starts behaving differently — whispers, guarded reactions, questions with edges. The character may not know exactly what has moved or to whom. Containment cost increases. |
| 3   | Exposed | The secret is out. Full exposure consequences fire. The character no longer spends resources on this secret — it is part of the world now. |

_Spread 3 is not a cliff edge. When the SG advances the track to Exposed, they describe how exposure unfolds in the fiction before consequences are applied. The player chooses the form of the Burden. The table decides how the world reacts._

**Design note — Spread 1–5:** The default track is three steps. For campaigns where secrets are central to the fiction, the SG and players may agree to extend individual secrets to a 1–5 Spread track. This creates longer containment arcs and higher cumulative cost (Weight + Spread at step 4 or 5 becomes a serious ongoing drain), but gives the table more room to work with slow-burning revelations. This is a per-secret decision, not a system-wide setting.

### Containment

Each arc, the player declares their containment spend and covers the cost. This is player-led. At arc end the player announces what they spent, confirms it meets the total, and either holds the track or marks the advance themselves.

Containment cost = Weight + current Spread. A Weight 2 secret at Spread 1 costs 3 resource points per arc. The same secret at Spread 2 costs 4. Resources may be combined freely in any proportion. The total must be met.

| **Resource** | **Points** | **What it represents** | **Corruption risk** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Downtime actions | 1 per action | Active management: stories maintained, trails obscured, witnesses checked on, evidence walked back. The character is doing the work personally. | None by itself. Corruption applies if the action involves something the character’s Conviction forbids. |
| Wealth points | 1 per point | Silence purchased: bribes, favors, legal arrangements, hired distraction. The character is paying for the world to look elsewhere. | Bribing witnesses or officials: Conviction check if the character’s Path forbids corrupting institutions. Failure = 1 Corruption. |
| Contact health steps | 1 per step of decay on one contact | A person who knows is being leaned on to stay quiet. Their relationship with the character degrades silently — no signal. A single contact can carry multiple secrets; each secret costs a health step independently. A contact carrying several secrets degrades fast and becomes dangerous when lost. | Coercing an unwilling contact: Conviction check. Failure = 1 Corruption. If a contact carrying this secret reaches Lost, Spread advances 1 step immediately regardless of other spending. |
| Anchor damage | 1 per track box (Standing or Stores) | The party’s home base absorbs cost — resources diverted, standing quietly spent, the Anchor’s reputation eroded by association. The party may not know what is draining their resources. | None by itself. The SG determines how the Anchor absorbs the damage fictionally. |

_Combining methods is expected for high-Weight secrets. A Weight 3 secret at Spread 2 costs 5 resource points per arc. That is a serious ongoing drain that should be felt at the table._

### Advancing the Spread Track

The player marks all Spread advances on their own sheet. The SG triggers advances through the world and announces when they do. The player then marks the step.

| **Trigger** | **Effect** | **Who marks it** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Containment failure | Arc end arrives and the full cost was not met. Spread advances 1 step. | Player — player declares the shortfall and marks it. |
| Contact lost through suppression | A contact carrying this secret reaches health 0. Spread advances 1 step immediately regardless of other spending that arc. | Player — player is already tracking contact health and marks on loss. |
| Taint spend | The SG spends Taint equal to the secret’s Weight. Spread advances 1 step. Cost scales with Weight — a Weight 3 secret resists casual pressure. | SG announces the spend and the fictional signal. Player marks the advance. |
| Fiction event | An in-play event makes concealment impossible. Advance is warranted by what just happened. | Table agrees it applies. Player marks the advance. |
| Cascade — linked secret exposes | A causally linked Secret reaches Spread 3. This secret’s Spread advances 1 step immediately. Causal links are SG-recorded at establishment and may not be known to the player until the cascade fires. | SG announces the cascade and its source. Player marks the advance. |

_When Spread advances to 2, the world’s awareness expresses itself through fiction. The SG does not name a specific figure or announce a track movement. Instead the world begins behaving differently around the character — a door slightly ajar that was closed before, a name said carefully, questions with edges. The character may sense that something has shifted without knowing what or where. This is the signal._

### Shared Secrets

When an act implicates multiple characters — the whole party was there, two characters made the decision together — the secret can be recorded as shared. A shared Secret uses the same mechanics as an individual one with three adjustments.

First: containment cost is still Weight + Spread per arc, but the party distributes the cost among themselves freely. Each character marks their share of the spend on their own sheet.

Second: any character involved can invoke the cascade rule as either source or destination. If one character’s individual secret is causally linked to the shared one, exposure of either can fire the cascade.

Third: when a shared Secret exposes, each involved character takes the Burden individually. The narrative consequence and Faction Scale shifts apply to the group. The Tide fires once.

_A shared Secret is more expensive to contain because more people are managing it. It is also more dangerous when it breaks — the consequences distribute but do not diminish._

### How Secrets End

A Secret ends when it is no longer a secret. That can happen through full Spread exposure, voluntary confession, or out-of-game retirement when a thread has run its course.

Voluntary confession is a valid path and may produce lighter consequences than full exposure — a character who comes forward before Spread reaches 3 has more control over the framing, the timing, and the audience. But confession is not absolution. The world still knows. The Burden still fires, scaled to how the SG reads the circumstances. Faction Scale shifts may be reduced if the confession was strategic and well-timed. The Tide fires as normal if the secret connects to one.

Some secrets outlive their holders. A Weight 3 secret carried for decades may never fully expose in the original character’s lifetime but still shapes the world — as historical residue, as a Tide that seeded and slowly moved, as something a descendant or successor inherits without knowing what they’re holding. The world does not forget on a schedule.

Out-of-game retirement: if a storyline ends, the secret’s thread dies, or the table agrees the fiction has moved past it, the secret can be quietly dropped without firing consequences. This is a collaborative decision. The SG and players close it together. It does not need a mechanical resolution — some things simply stop mattering, not because the world forgot but because the story moved on.

### The World Remembers

Containment works on people. It does not work on the world.

Animals were present. Objects carry the impression of what happened near them. In settings where spirits walk, land remembers, or digital systems log, the texture of what occurred cannot be removed by paying the right people. A psychic reads the old hay fork. A druid asks the crows. An archivist queries the network. These are not containable through normal means. The character who believes they left no witnesses is not wrong about the people. They are wrong about the world.

The SG tracks this as world-state and expresses it through Taint spends and fiction. No special rule covers it. It is how the engine works. The world moves whether or not the character is paying attention.

Setting supplements define the specific mechanisms. Wrath & Woe names its spirits and what they can be asked. Drakorath describes what the old bloodlines carry. Ghost Code specifies what the network logs and how long retention lasts. SG guidance for each supplement should specify: what non-human witnesses exist in this setting, and what does it take to access their knowledge.

### Corruption and Secrets

The acts that create Secrets often generate Corruption. Murder, betrayal, defiance of binding oaths, Conviction-breaking choices — these are both the kinds of things worth hiding and the kinds of things the engine already tracks. A character who acquires a significant Secret has often already paid in Corruption for the act that made it necessary.

Suppression generates additional Corruption through the containment methods themselves. Bribing officials. Coercing witnesses. Destroying evidence. Each carries a Conviction check. A character whose Path forbids these methods faces a specific tension: the cheaper forms of containment are the ones that cost them who they are.

There is no direct mechanical link between Corruption and Spread. The connection runs through the Taint pool. Corruption feeds Taint. Taint can be spent to advance Spread. The machine is already built — the Secret system uses it.

### When a Secret Fully Exposes

When Spread reaches its final step, all four consequences fire simultaneously, scaled to the secret’s Weight.

| **Consequence** | **What fires** |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative | The world reacts to what it now knows. The SG describes how. People act on the information. Relationships shift. Doors close. This always fires. |
| Burden | The player takes a Burden at dot rating equal to Weight. Player proposes the Burden type — Infamous, Indebted, and Pariah are most common. The SG confirms the type is plausible given the fiction and that the dot rating matches the secret’s Weight. Standard Burden rules apply from here. |
| Faction Scale shift | Any faction with stakes in the Secret shifts their Social Scale against the character by 1 step per Weight point in relevant interactions. This is permanent until directly addressed through sustained fictional effort. |
| Tide | If the secret connects to an active Tide, it advances 1 stage. If no Tide exists and Weight is 2 or higher, the SG may seed a new one from the exposure. Not every exposure generates a Tide — only when the fiction makes it natural. |

| **Weight** | **Burden dot rating** | **Common types** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1   | •   | Infamous •, Indebted •, Phobia • |

### Edges and Secrets

There is no default mechanical benefit to holding a secret. Specific Edges can interact with the system — granting leverage when operating from hidden knowledge, reducing containment costs through established networks, or providing a Luck token when a secret is put at active risk during play. These are Edge effects, not baseline Secrets mechanics. Characters who invest in discretion, intelligence networks, or social camouflage are investing in tools that make secrets survivable. Characters who don’t are carrying the full cost.

### EXAMPLE — Sir Gregor and the Village

_Sir Gregor, passing through a village outside Goldenfields, is shown a writ from a local lord compelling knights to assist with a bandit problem. He ignores the writ and leaves. Growing increasingly anxious that the lord will discover his defiance, he returns to the village and kills everyone there — or believes he does. He rides away with two secrets and a Corruption point he has already paid._

**Secret 1: Defied the Lord’s Writ**

Weight 3, Spread 1. This lord regards defiance as a graver offense than violence. A defied writ puts Gregor’s standing, his title, and his continued operation under the lord’s sphere at risk. The SG sets Weight 3 and tells Gregor. The writ defiance is already a Weight 3 problem before anyone died.

Containment cost this arc (Weight 3 + Spread 1 = 4): Gregor covers 2 points with Wealth — payments to the lord’s chamberlain, who has been useful before. He covers 2 points with Downtime — personally checking that no messenger carried the original writ back to court. The chamberlain’s contact health ticks down 1 step with no signal to Gregor. He is now Distant. Gregor does not know this yet.

**Secret 2: Massacred the Village**

Weight 2, Spread 1. The SG sets Weight 2. Not because the act was less severe — it was not — but because its immediate political consequences, if exposed, are more navigable than the lord’s response to defiance. A massacre can be attributed to bandits. Defiance cannot be reframed.

Causal link recorded by SG: Secret 2 is causally linked to Secret 1. If Secret 1 reaches Spread 3, the lord’s investigation into Gregor’s movements will find the village. Secret 2’s Spread advances 1 step when Secret 1 exposes. Gregor does not know this link exists.

Containment cost this arc (Weight 2 + Spread 1 = 3): Gregor believes he has no witnesses. He puts 1 Downtime into walking the site and 2 Wealth into paying a factor to spread a bandit story through the surrounding area. The SG notes this privately.

**What Gregor doesn’t know:**

A farmer named Aldous was in the far field. He saw smoke and heard things he couldn’t explain. He told his merchant cousin in the next town. The merchant cousin is cautious and has hired a traveling scholar with a reputation for unusual inquiries.

The animals were there. A druid passing through three weeks later stops at a stream, speaks with the crows, and learns the shape of what happened without a name attached yet.

None of this is containable through Gregor’s spending. The SG is accumulating Taint from Gregor’s Corruption — the massacre itself triggered a Conviction-linked Corruption point. The chamberlain coercion was another. The SG banks quietly.

**Arc Two:**

Secret 1 is holding at Spread 1. Containment cost is still 4. Gregor covers it again. The chamberlain is now at Distant (health 1). One more arc of being tapped and he reaches Strained — and a Strained contact will give Gregor up for the right offer.

Secret 2: the SG spends 2 Taint (Weight 2 cost) against Secret 2’s track. The SG announces: “Spread on the village secret advances.” Gregor marks Spread 2 on his sheet. The SG does not name a figure. Instead: a contact reaches out mid-session with a warning. Someone in the next town is asking questions about a knight’s livery matching Gregor’s. The world is behaving differently. Gregor does not know how much it knows.

Secret 2 now costs 4 per arc (Weight 2 + Spread 2). Gregor is paying 4 on Secret 1 and 4 on Secret 2. Eight resource points per arc, and one of his primary contacts is nearly spent.

_If Gregor does nothing, both tracks continue advancing. Secret 2 reaches Spread 3 first. When it does, the causal cascade fires: Secret 1’s Spread advances 1 step immediately. The lord’s investigation into the massacre leads directly to the ignored writ. Both secrets expose in the same arc. Four consequences fire twice. Gregor’s Weight 3 secret fires a Burden at ••• and a Faction Scale shift that ends his standing with the lord’s court._

_He could have helped with the bandits._

### Secrets on the Character Sheet

The Secrets section records each secret’s name, Weight, current Spread (with boxes equal to the agreed track length, default 3), and the current arc’s containment plan broken down by resource type. A notation line records which specific contacts are currently being used for containment so contact health decay can be tracked accurately. Causal links are SG-side only and do not appear on the player sheet.

The player updates this section in real time during and between sessions. Containment plans can be adjusted at the start of each arc as circumstances change — a contact who has gone Distant may no longer be a viable containment resource, and the player needs to redistribute the cost.

When a single contact is carrying more than one secret, note each secret separately in the contact’s notation line. Each costs a health step independently, so the decay adds up fast and the player needs to see it clearly before committing to arc spend.

_Example notation — Contacts in use this arc:_

_Chamberlain (Secret 1: Writ — 1 health step this arc; Secret 2: Village — 1 health step this arc) → 2 steps total this arc → currently Warm, will reach Distant at arc end_

_A contact at Distant carrying two secrets is one arc away from Strained. A contact at Strained carrying two secrets will reach Lost mid-arc if tapped again. When that contact goes, both Spread tracks advance._

## LOSS AND CONSEQUENCE

Loss in the Willowlight Engine is never arbitrary. It is always the result of choices — either the choices that led to the loss, or the choice of which form the loss takes.

### Two Forms of Agency

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Form** | **Description** |
| Prospective agency | The player made the choices that produced the loss. Neglecting a contact, defying a lord, burning a village to keep a secret. The fiction produced a consequence the player set in motion. The SG did not reach in and take something. |
| Retrospective agency | When loss occurs, the player chooses its specific form from a consequence menu. Losing a lord's backing might mean losing Armory access, or losing Status, or losing a Retainer. The SG sets the scope. The player chooses the form. |

### Signal Before Loss

Before a character's personally managed resource degrades or is lost, the player receives a clear signal. 'Another session without contact and your relationship with Corvin will deteriorate.' 'The lord's patience is visibly wearing thin.' The player is choosing to let it happen — not being surprised by it.

Silent removal of a personally-managed resource is never appropriate. If the signal was not given, the loss does not happen yet. This principle applies to resources the player actively manages: connection health tracks, property upkeep, access Edges that require ongoing relationships. It does not apply to world events. The world moves without announcing itself.

### Scope of Loss

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Resource** | **How it can be lost** |
| Connections | Health track reaches zero. Connection is lost. Can be rebuilt through sustained effort and major fictional work. |
| Access Edges | Access revoked by the source. An armory contact cut off by a lord, a guild membership revoked. |
| Property Edges | A safe house burned, a ship sunk, a manor seized. Major consequence, requires significant fictional setup. |
| Status Edges | Title stripped, reputation destroyed, rank removed. Usually Social conflict outcomes or political consequences. |
| Skill tier | In rare, severe circumstances: prolonged incapacitation, traumatic injury. Player chooses which Interest or Hobby atrophies. |
| Attribute point | Exceptional severity only. Permanent physical or mental degradation. Player chooses the affected attribute. |

_The scope of loss creates a natural power ceiling without an XP cap. Characters who accumulate resources through play are also accumulating things that can be threatened, damaged, or lost. The Taint pool has more targets. Luck has more uses. The game remains interesting at high XP because what matters isn't just what you have — it's what you're trying to protect._

A character with the Poor Burden (•–•••) must pay 1 wealth point per dot per arc to maintain basic equipment and lifestyle. If unpaid, they suffer −1 to Physical defense TN (deteriorating equipment) and −1 to Social rolls (visible poverty) per dot of Poor. These penalties clear the moment the wealth point is paid.

## TIME

The Willowlight Engine uses two separate time tracks that serve different purposes.

### Tactical and Narrative Intervals

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Interval** | **Duration (approx.)** | **Rules interactions** |
| Round | ~10 seconds | Basic unit of conflict. All participants act once per round. |
| Scene | Variable | Conditions lasting 'until end of scene' clear here. Recovery between conflicts happens at scene breaks. |
| Arc | Multiple sessions | Milestone XP and arc completion bonuses trigger here. Tide advancement happens at arc end. Some afflictions resolve at arc boundaries. |

Day, week, month, and year are real-world time references used in affliction descriptions, crafting timelines, and setting-specific rules. They require no special mechanical framework. The SG determines how much real time passes between scenes and arcs based on the fiction.

_The engine deliberately avoids a unified time-tracking subsystem. Tactical intervals have rules because they interact with the action economy. Real-time intervals are narrative description. Conflating the two creates unnecessary complexity._

## RESEARCH AND PREPARATION

Preparation activities — investigating a target, gathering information about a location, digging up leverage before a negotiation — are supported through the existing mechanics rather than a separate system.

### Preparing for Mental Conflict

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Preparation method** | **Result** | **Limit** |
| Brief (one scene of investigation) | +1 to your first Assess action in the conflict | Once per conflict |
| Moderate (dedicated downtime or extended session play) | +1 to all Assess actions in the conflict | Once per conflict |
| Extensive (multiple sessions or a full arc of investigation) | +1 to all Assess actions AND Analyze does not count toward repeat penalty | Once per arc against this target |

### Preparing for Social Conflict

Leverage gathered through dedicated investigation rather than in-scene discovery starts at one tier higher than it otherwise would.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Preparation method** | **Result** | **Limit** |
| Brief research | Generate Minor leverage on the target (if any exists to find) | Once per target per arc |
| Moderate investigation | Generate Major leverage, or upgrade existing Minor to Major | Once per target per arc |
| Deep investigation (scene-level) | Generate Devastating leverage, or uncover a specific secret that may be more valuable than formal leverage | SG determines what's findable |

Investigating a target always carries risk. A complication on a preparation roll may mean the investigation was noticed, a source was compromised, or the target now knows someone is looking into them. The SG should make this consequence feel like a natural result of the fiction, not a punishment for trying to prepare.

### Downtime Preparation

Preparation that doesn't warrant a full scene can be handled through the downtime system. Spending 1 downtime point on preparation gives +1 to one specific roll type during the next session. The player declares in advance what they prepared for: 'I spent downtime researching the cult's ritual practices' means +1 to rolls involving cult lore or ritual recognition next session.

# PART SEVEN: THE STORYGUIDE

_How to run the engine at the table_

## THE STORYGUIDE'S ROLE

_\[ART: SG at the head of a table, notes spread out, three players leaning in — warmly lit, engaged\]_

The Storyguide is the world's voice. Your job is to make the world feel real, to play every character in it who isn't a player character, and to let the fiction respond honestly to what the players do. You are not the players' adversary. You are not their babysitter. You are the person who makes their choices matter.

The Willowlight Engine is designed to reduce your cognitive load. Streamlined NPC stat blocks, a player-facing roll structure, and a Taint pool that scales automatically with the session's stakes mean you can spend your attention on the narrative rather than the math.

This section covers the SG-specific mechanics and philosophy of the engine. It assumes you've read Parts One through Five and have a working understanding of the rules.

### What the SG Controls

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **SG tool** | **What it does** |
| Taint pool | Your primary active resource. Spend it to apply pressure. Build it through PC Corruption gains. |
| Target Numbers | Set before rolls. Your main tool for expressing fictional resistance. |
| NPC behavior | You control what NPCs do, say, want, and feel. The dice determine outcomes. |
| Tides | World threats that advance based on banked Taint. The engine of consequence between sessions. |
| Consequence menus | You set the severity tier for Break and Shattered Hearth consequences. Players choose the form. |
| World events | Things that happen off-screen, discovered through play. Not announced. |

### What the SG Doesn't Control

The SG never controls the outcome of player rolls. The SG never takes player agency over their character's choices or consequences. The SG does not decide what form a player's Shattered Hearth consequence takes — only the severity tier. The SG does not silently remove player resources without the Signal Before Loss principle.

These are not restrictions on good storytelling. They are the structural commitments that make the game work. A table that trusts the SG to respect these principles can go to genuinely dark and challenging places together, because everyone knows the rules.

## SETTING TARGET NUMBERS

Target Numbers are your most important tool. They are set before the roll — not adjusted after. Setting the TN before the dice hit the table gives players the information they need to make informed choices. Adjusting after undermines that.

The TN communicates the fiction. A TN of 8 says 'this is within reach for a competent person.' A TN of 16 says 'this is at the edge of what's possible.' A TN you set based on the fictional context tells the player something true about the world they're navigating.

### The TN Ladder

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **TN** | **Difficulty** | **Use it when...** |
| 8   | Easy | The character is capable and the situation is cooperative. Stakes are real even if the skill is common. |
| 10  | Moderate | A genuine challenge. The standard for most professional-level tasks. |
| 12  | Challenging | Requires real skill or the right approach. The wrong approach may make success impossible. |
| 14  | Hard | At the edge of capability. Reserve for significant story moments. |
| 16  | Severe | Exceptional difficulty. Use sparingly — it should feel like a genuine test. |
| 18  | Legendary | Near the limit of mortal capability. Most characters will fail more often than they succeed. |

### TN Modifiers

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Raise the TN when...** | **Lower the TN when...** |
| The character is working against their nature or training | The character has specific preparation or information |
| The target is specifically prepared or resistant | Allies are providing active support |
| The context makes the action harder than normal | The target is distracted, weakened, or compromised |
| Previous actions have damaged this character's credibility | The character has leverage or advantage that directly applies |
| The environment is actively hostile to this approach | The context is unusually favorable |

### When Not to Call for a Roll

Not every action needs a roll. Rolls are for uncertain outcomes where the stakes matter. If the outcome is obvious — the character succeeds because they're clearly capable and there's no meaningful opposition — narrate the success. If the outcome is obvious failure — the character attempts something genuinely beyond their ability or the fiction makes it impossible — narrate the failure.

The most common mistake new SGs make is calling for rolls on everything. This dilutes the significance of the dice. When a roll happens, it should feel like something. If it doesn't, you probably didn't need the roll.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Don't roll when...** | **Example** |
| The outcome is obvious given the character's capability | A trained soldier intimidates an unarmed merchant. Just narrate it. |
| The task is routine and there are no meaningful stakes | A scholar identifies a common herb. No roll. |
| Failure would be dramatically inappropriate right now | The lock pick at the start of the exciting chase scene. Success is the interesting part. |
| The roll would only slow down established momentum | The climax scene's opening action. Let it land. |

## RUNNING CONFLICT

Conflict in the Willowlight Engine is structured but not rigid. The action economy gives you a framework, not a straitjacket. Your job in conflict is to make the opposition feel real, to play NPCs as if they have genuine goals and survival instincts, and to keep the fiction moving alongside the mechanics.

### Before Conflict Starts

Know what each NPC wants before conflict begins. Not just 'defeat the players' — that's not a goal a person has. A guard captain wants to protect the garrison and go home. A negotiator wants to close the deal and demonstrate his worth to his employer. A cult lieutenant wants to complete the ritual and prove herself to her superior.

When you know what NPCs want, you can play them making decisions under pressure, not just executing mechanical actions. An NPC who is losing a fight does not keep fighting until their TN hits zero unless they have a strong reason to do so. Most people — most creatures — concede when they understand they've lost.

### Using Taint in Conflict

Taint is your active lever in conflict. Spend it to create pressure and test the players' resources. Don't save it — Taint that isn't spent is likely to be banked toward Tides, which is also valuable, but in-session spending creates immediate drama.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Taint spend** | **When to use it** |
| Force Luck swap (1) | When a player rolls well on something that matters. The cost of their success should feel real. |
| Raise NPC TN (1–2) | When an NPC has a specific reason to be harder to affect right now. Give a fictional reason first. |
| Add complication (1) | When a success happens that should have a cost, but no cost was built into the roll. Layer something on top. |
| Tier point on NPC action (1) | When a player fails a defense roll and the NPC should capitalize on that. |

### Playing NPCs in Conflict

NPCs in conflict are trying to accomplish something, not trying to reduce player hit points. Play them with that orientation. A negotiator who is losing a social conflict looks for a way to reframe the conversation, not a way to deal more Composure damage. A soldier who is outmatched looks for an exit, not a way to roll one more time.

NPCs should use their Traits. If a Trait says 'Patient — never deploys leverage immediately,' the NPC waits even when waiting costs them. Traits are character details, not just mechanics. Playing them consistently is what makes NPCs feel like people rather than stat blocks.

### Ending Conflict

Watch for the moment when an NPC should concede. The fiction usually makes it obvious: they've lost two-thirds of their group, or their leverage has been neutralized, or the player character just did something that genuinely frightened them. When you see that moment, let the NPC concede. Don't force them to fight to zero unless their motivation demands it.

Concession is not failure. An NPC who concedes gracefully — who retreats with their remaining dignity intact — is more interesting in future scenes than one who was beaten unconscious. They remember. The world remembers.

## MANAGING THE TAINT POOL

### Session Start

The Taint pool starts each session at a baseline. The standard formula: risk rating of the session's primary threat (1–5) plus the number of players. A low-stakes session in a safe location might start at 4 or 5. A session at the front of an active Tide might start at 7 or 8.

Set the starting Taint openly. Players knowing the SG has 7 Taint at the start of a session is not information they can use to avoid consequences — it is information that makes the pressure feel real.

### Building Taint During the Session

Taint builds through player Corruption gains. Every time a character gains Corruption, the SG gains 1 Taint. This means the session's Taint economy is directly connected to how hard the players push their characters.

A session where the players are cautious and avoid risky Aspect uses will have low Taint growth. A session where the players are aggressive and make costly choices will have high Taint growth. The system naturally escalates when the fiction escalates.

### Spending During the Session

Spend Taint in the moment. When a player rolls well on something that matters, force a Luck swap. When an NPC should be harder to crack right now, spend to raise their TN. When a success should have a cost and none is built in, add a complication.

The rhythm should feel like pressure — not punishment. Players should feel the Taint pool as a force that responds to their choices, not as arbitrary interference. When you spend Taint, give a fictional reason. 'The guard captain has seen this kind of approach before' is better than a TN raise that appears from nowhere.

### End-of-Session Resolution

At session end, you have two options for remaining Taint: spend it on off-screen consequences, or bank it toward Tides.

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **End-of-session spend** | **Cost** | **Effect** |
| Faction escalation | 1–2 Taint | A faction advances a plan, deploys resources, or reaches a new position. Players discover this through play, not announcement. |
| Relationship / resource pressure | 1 Taint | A world event damages a connection health track or a resource. Not announced — players discover when they next interact. |
| Resource deterioration | 1 Taint | A property or access Edge shows signs of strain. Discovered, not narrated. |
| Corruption pressure | 1 Taint | An environmental Corruption source intensifies, raising its potency by 1 for the next session. |
| Bank toward Tides | Any amount | Unspent Taint moves to the arc bank. Used to advance Tides at arc end. |

Taint not spent or banked at session end is lost. This is not waste — a session where the players managed the pressure well and left the SG with unspent Taint is a session where the players won something real.

### Arc-End Tide Advancement

At arc end, the SG spends the arc bank on Tide advancement. Each Tide milestone has a cost (typically 1–3 banked Taint). The arc bank is spent, Tides advance, and the world changes. Players may discover these changes immediately or over the course of the next arc, depending on how visible the consequences are.

Whatever remains in the arc bank at arc end is lost. The bank resets. The next arc begins with a fresh Taint pool and a world that has moved.

## TIDES IN PRACTICE

Building and running Tides is where long-term SG craft lives. A well-built Tide feels inevitable in retrospect and surprising in the moment. A poorly built Tide feels arbitrary. The difference is almost always preparation.

### Building a Tide

Start with the completion milestone — what happens if the players never intervene. Make it genuinely bad. Then work backward through 3 to 4 intermediate milestones.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Tide design question** | **What to decide** |
| What is this threat? | A faction, a person, a situation, a force of nature. Give it a name and a goal. |
| What does it want? | The completion state. Write this first. Be specific. |
| How does it get there? | 3–4 intermediate milestones. Each should feel like real progress, not just flavor. |
| What can players find? | At each milestone, what clues are visible to attentive players? |
| What would stop it? | One or two specific interventions that would set the Tide back or end it. |

### Running Multiple Tides

A typical campaign has 2 to 4 active Tides at once. Tides should have different timeframes — one might complete in a single arc if unaddressed, another might be a slow-burning multi-arc threat. The variation creates natural pacing rhythm.

Not every Tide needs player engagement. Some Tides should advance whether or not the players notice, creating a world that changes without their direction. This is what makes discovery matter. When a Tide the players didn't know about reaches its completion, their reaction — 'we should have seen this coming' or 'we were warned and didn't act' — is the whole point.

### Tide Signals and Discovery

Each Tide should have visible signs at each milestone that attentive players can find. These are not announcements — they are clues available to players who are paying attention. Design them to make sense in retrospect: when a Tide completes, players looking back should be able to trace the warning signs they missed.

Never punish players for missing signals. The world moves whether they watch it or not. That's consequence, not punishment. The distinction matters at the table.

## RUNNING SECRETS

A Secret is a live obligation, not backstory. The player tracks Weight, Spread, and containment spend. The SG tracks causal links and world-witness state. Keep these roles clean — the player manages what their character knows; the SG manages what the world knows.

### Setting Weight

Weight measures consequence severity, not moral severity. Set it by asking one question: who finds out, and what can they do about it?

A Weight 1 secret inconveniences. A Weight 2 secret endangers standing or relationships that operate at scale. A Weight 3 secret ends things — titles, alliances, freedom. When in doubt, set Weight lower. A secret that turns out to be more consequential than expected can be re-evaluated. A secret set too heavy at the start creates unsustainable containment costs before the story has earned them.

Tell the player the Weight immediately. It is their information to have.

### Recording Causal Links

Causal links live on the SG side only. They are the connections between secrets that the character cannot see.

Record them simply: 'If Secret A reaches Spread 3, Secret B advances 1 step.' A single sentence per link is enough. You do not need a web — just the specific cascade that matters. Most secrets have zero or one causal link. A character who has done several interconnected things may have two or three.

Do not reveal causal links to players. The discovery that two secrets were connected all along is a story moment. Don't spend it early.

### World-Witness Escalation

The world witnesses through people, animals, objects, and in some settings the land itself. When you decide the world has learned something — through a Taint spend or a fiction event — give it a fictional face before advancing the track.

| **Spread step** | **How to signal it** |
| --- | --- |
| Spread 1 → 2 | A named person or group is behaving differently. No accusation. A guard who used to nod now watches. A merchant who used to deal is suddenly unavailable. The character notices a change; they don't yet know the cause. |
| Spread 2 → 3 | The shape of the secret is visible. Someone asks a question that couldn't be coincidence. A faction representative requests a meeting. The character now knows something is out — they just don't know how much or to whom. |
| Spread 3 | Exposure. Describe the moment specifically. Name who speaks first and what they say. This is a scene, not a table entry. |

### What the SG Doesn't Do

Don't track containment spend — that's the player's job. Don't calculate whether the arc cost was met — the player declares it and marks the advance if it wasn't. Don't use Secrets as punishment for characters you want to pressure. Use Taint, which has a cost that scales with Weight. A Weight 3 secret costs 3 Taint to advance — that's real pressure, and it comes out of a finite pool.

## SESSION STRUCTURE

The Willowlight Engine doesn't mandate a specific session structure, but the following framework has proven useful across different play styles. It is a suggestion, not a requirement.

### Before the Session

Review your active Tides. Know each one's current state and what would advance it.

Prepare the NPCs the players are likely to encounter. Know what each one wants, fears, and will concede.

Set your session Taint pool based on risk rating.

Know the answer to 'what happens if the players do nothing today?' If nothing changes without player action, you may not have enough active Tides.

Have 2–3 TN values pre-set for likely scenes. You don't need to plan everything, but the obvious first move of each scene should have a TN ready.

### During the Session

Open each scene with a clear situation. Not 'you are standing in a room' but 'the door is open, there are voices inside, and the voices do not sound friendly.'

When players Assess, answer honestly and specifically. The quality of Assess answers is one of the clearest signals of SG craft. An Assess answer that gives real information the players can use is an investment in the scene. An Assess answer that hedges and withholds is a withdrawal.

Track your Taint pool visibly. Knowing when you have a lot or a little changes how you make decisions.

When conflict ends, narrate the consequence. Don't let the mechanical resolution happen in a vacuum. The TN was met or it wasn't. Something changed in the fiction. Say what it is.

Watch for the moment when an NPC should concede. Don't force them to fight to zero.

### After the Session

Spend remaining Taint on end-of-session consequences or bank toward Tides.

Update your Tide progression based on what happened. Note any that the players directly engaged — those may be slowed or halted.

Note any connections that were neglected, misused, or strengthened.

Ask the players one question: what was the best moment? This takes thirty seconds and tells you what to do more of.

### Pacing Across Domains

The three conflict domains play at different speeds and create different kinds of tension. Physical conflict is fast and immediate. Mental conflict rewards careful setup and has slow momentum once rolling. Social conflict is relational and often resolves through concession rather than track depletion.

Sessions that are entirely one domain tend to feel flat. Physical conflict without social texture feels like a dungeon bash. Social conflict without physical stakes feels low-consequence. Mental conflict without other domains supporting it can feel disconnected. Mix them, even within single scenes.

The most memorable scenes often involve two or three domains simultaneously: a negotiation conducted while both parties are armed and aware of the physical option, an argument happening in a burning building, a social performance that is also a covert intelligence operation.

## BUILDING LEGENDS

_\[ART: A figure seen from behind, standing at the edge of something vast — the scale wrong, the presence undeniable\]_

Legends are the setting's defining figures — the powers that shaped the world before the players arrived. Building a Legend is not a mechanical exercise. It is a world-building exercise that happens to have mechanical expression.

Build Legends between sessions. If you find yourself building a Legend during a session, something has gone wrong with preparation. A Legend should be a character you know well before the players encounter them.

### The Legend Design Questions

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **Question** | **What to decide** |
| What did this Legend do? | Not 'they were powerful' but 'this specific thing happened because of them.' What changed in the world because this person existed? |
| What do they want now? | How has that changed from when they were at the height of their power? Power changes what people want. Knowing the current want is more important than knowing the origin. |
| What do they fear? | What would it cost them to lose, and what would they do to prevent that loss? Fear is the engine of dramatic behavior. |
| How do they see the party? | Pawns? Threats? Potential allies? Irritants? The Legend's read of the party shapes every scene they're in together. |
| What is the encounter about? | Not 'defeat the Boss' but 'what story does this encounter tell?' The mechanical result should feel like a consequence of a story, not the story itself. |

### Legend Scale

Legends frequently operate at Scale 3 to 5 in their primary domain. This places them qualitatively outside normal engagement. A character trying to fight a Scale 5 Legend directly will find the TN unreachable without a specific vulnerability or approach.

This is the point. Engaging a Legend narratively before engaging them mechanically is almost always correct. Find the vulnerability. Change the terms. Bring something that makes the Scale gap irrelevant. The Legend's Scale is not a barrier to overcome by rolling big numbers — it is a signal that the story needs a different approach.

### Legend Stat Blocks

Legends use full PC-equivalent character creation. Attributes, Skills, Edges, Aspects, Conviction, Path — all built using the same rules as player characters. Setting supplements define Legend-exclusive Aspects that go beyond the core engine's trees.

The mechanical core is less important than knowing who this person is. A Legend with a detailed personality and clear wants is interesting in every scene. A Legend with optimal mechanics but no defined character is only interesting in combat.

_Build the personality before you build the stat block. The stat block should express the personality, not define it._

## SAFETY AND TONE

The Willowlight Engine is designed to support stories with genuine stakes, moral complexity, and real consequences. These qualities require a table that feels safe enough to engage with difficult material honestly. Safety tools are not a restriction on good play. They are a prerequisite for it.

### Before the First Session

Have a conversation with your players before play begins. It doesn't need to be formal or extensive. The questions to address:

What kind of story does everyone want to tell? What tone are people hoping for?

Are there subjects or content types that would make play uncomfortable or harmful for anyone at the table?

How does the table want to handle it when someone needs to step back from a scene?

### The X-Card

The X-Card is a simple safety tool: an index card with an X on it, placed on the table where everyone can reach it. If anyone taps or holds up the X-Card, the current scene stops or changes, no questions asked. No explanation required.

The X-Card works because it removes the social cost of using it. 'I need to stop this scene' is easier when there's a physical object that says 'this is normal' rather than requiring someone to speak up in the middle of play.

The Willowlight Engine's concession mechanics already provide in-fiction off-ramps for challenging conflict. The X-Card provides an out-of-fiction equivalent for challenging content. They serve different purposes and do not replace each other.

### Tone Calibration

The Willowlight Engine supports a range of tones. The same rules that run a horror campaign run a political thriller run a swashbuckling adventure. The tone lives in the SG's choices and the table's social contract, not in the mechanics.

Calibrate tone through the consequences you emphasize. A table that wants dark, grinding tension gets Corruption that accumulates slowly and Shattered Hearth consequences that stick. A table that wants heroic adventure gets Corruption that recovers readily and Breaks that feel dramatic but not permanent. Both are valid uses of the engine.

### Player Character Death

The Willowlight Engine does not include automatic death mechanics. Health tracks reaching zero produces Felled, Broken, or Fallen states — serious but not fatal. Death is a specific narrative choice, not a mechanical outcome.

Settings may activate lethality through the Dying condition — a per-scene clock that creates real death stakes when the setting's tone calls for them. The core engine defaults to no automatic death trigger. Tables that want death as a realistic possibility should activate the Dying condition explicitly and discuss it in the pre-session conversation.

When a character dies, the player should have the most agency possible over how it happens. Death that emerges from meaningful choices the player made is meaningful. Death that emerges from an unlucky sequence of rolls is not.

An Enemy is a named specific person, organization, or faction that has turned its attention toward the character with hostile intent. It is not abstract opposition — it is someone in particular, with a name, a reason, and resources proportional to their position in the world.

Enemy can be taken at character creation, representing a pre-existing nemesis. At creation it grants 1 Edge dot per Burden dot, the same as all other Burdens. The fiction that created the enemy — the slight, the theft, the betrayal, the accident — is the player's to define.

| **Dot** | **State** | **Effect** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| •   | Aware | The enemy knows the character exists and has noted them as a potential problem. In the enemy's sphere of influence, the SG may spend 1 Taint to add a complication to any roll as interference — a contact who is suddenly guarded, a door that was open that is now closed. |
| ••  | Invested | The enemy is gathering information. They have a picture of the character's habits, contacts, and movements. The SG may interfere once per session without spending Taint. Any roll that touches the enemy's network suffers −1. |
| ••• | Pursuing | The enemy is actively moving against the character. Once per arc, a scene involves the enemy or their agents directly — the SG introduces this, not the players. The interference is no longer passive. |
| •••• | Focused | The character is a primary target. The enemy has deployed real resources. Their Social Scale is treated as 1 step higher in all interactions involving the character. The enemy may act as a minor Tide — the SG may spend banked Taint to advance their agenda against the character between sessions. |
| ••••• | Defining | The enemy operates as a full Tide directed specifically at the character. They have milestones, resources, and a completion state. The character's life is shaped around this conflict whether they want it to be or not. |
